<LessonSummary>

- "Writing better" means using the AI on your draft, not having it write the text for you.
- Three patterns that keep your voice: **your draft, the AI's critique** (diagnosis, not rewriting), **more versions** (you take pieces, never a whole one), **emotional temperature** (you check the tone before sending).

You have an email to send. The deadline is ten minutes away, the
right tone won't come, and you're staring at a blank page.

You can ask the AI to write it for you and paste what it gives
you. Or you can ask it to help you write it better yourself. They
look the same, but they aren't. The first option returns a text
that would suit anyone with that generic problem: polite and
correct, but with no fingerprint. The second starts from an
attempt of yours, even a clumsy one, and works on it.

This lesson is about the second option. The texts that come out
look like yours because they are, and over time you become a
sharper reader of your own writing.

## Three patterns that keep your voice

### 1. Your draft, the AI's critique

Write the first version yourself, badly, fast, without rereading
it. The imperfect draft forces the AI to react to your ideas
instead of generating its own. Then you hand it to the AI to tell
you what isn't working, not to rewrite it.

> *"Here's my email. Does it feel too long? Where am I ambiguous?
> Is there a spot where I slide from polite to passive-aggressive
> without noticing?"*

What you get isn't a new text, it's a diagnosis: three or four
observations pinned to specific sentences. From there you rewrite,
choosing what to keep, cut, and move. The voice stays yours,
because the scaffolding stays yours. If you like, ask for the
critique in layers: first clarity, then tone, then length. They
are three different diagnoses, and separating them shows you what
to change first.

An example. I need to tell a client about a delay, my draft feels
bureaucratic but I can't say why. I ask only for the diagnosis.

The three observations are the map of what you'll change when you
rewrite.

### 2. More versions, none whole

Instead of asking for *the* right version, ask for three or four
calibrated differently, and don't take any of them whole.

> *"Give me three versions of this email asking for a refund. One
> direct and dry, one diplomatic, one formal. Three or four
> sentences each, no more."*

The labels matter: "three versions" with nothing else often gives
three near-identical variants. Different names (*"direct"*,
*"diplomatic"*, *"formal"*) force the AI to move the register in
recognizable directions.

The value is in seeing how the same thing changes shape and in
working out which register sounds like you. Usually it's none of
the three but a hybrid: the opening of the first, the middle of
the second, the close of the third. Read the versions aloud and
mark where a sentence slides off your tongue without a snag: that's
where your way of speaking is. If one version is already almost
right, tweak a sentence instead of building a mosaic.

A variant: paste two or three past texts of yours that you're
happy with and ask *"pick up the cadence of these texts, not their
content"*. The AI catches some tics of your rhythm, like where you
break sentences.

### 3. Emotional temperature

The third pattern uses one of the AI's strengths: reading the tone
of a text. It has seen a huge number of texts in which people
comment on the tone they received (*"it feels cold"*, *"it's
passive-aggressive"*) and it recognizes the surface signals that
cause it: an ironic *"certainly"*, a formal close that lands as
distant, a slightly wrong adjective.

> *"Read this email and tell me what tone it puts across. Does it
> sound cold? Passive-aggressive? I want to say no to a coworker
> without making them feel brushed off."*

It's most useful when the stakes are high: saying no to someone
who asked you for something, negotiating a raise, answering
feedback that hurt, delivering bad news. There the tone is the
message. It works in other languages too, with a caveat: the AI
sometimes applies the norms of another culture to yours (a *"cold"*
in American English isn't a cold in Italian).

<ChatDemo
  user={`I want to say no to Marco, a coworker, without making him feel brushed off. Read this email and tell me what tone it puts across:

"Hi Marco, thanks for thinking of me, but at the moment I can't take on this project. I hope you understand. Let me know if you need anything else. Luca"`}
  ai={`It sounds polite, but a bit distant. Two passages work against your intention.

"I hope you understand" right after a refusal can read as an early shutdown, as if you didn't want to discuss it.

"Let me know if you need anything else" at the end of a no is boilerplate: what else would Marco need? You've just told him you're not taking the project.

If Marco is a coworker you work with often, give the real reason for the no and drop the generic close. For example: "Thanks for thinking of me. I'm buried in deadlines right now and wouldn't be able to give the project the attention it deserves. If you need a hand in June, give me a shout."`}
/>

The diagnosis can also be wrong: the AI sometimes reads
exaggerated tones into neutral texts. If a reading surprises you,
ask *"what in the text makes you see this?"* and check the
passages it cites. If they don't hold up, discard the diagnosis.

Pattern 1 asks for a technical critique (clarity, structure,
length), this one for a reading of the emotional register. In
delicate cases run them one after the other, separately.

## The average voice

The *average voice* is the register the AI uses when you give it
no instructions: polite, correct, polished, interchangeable. It has
seen a huge number of work texts and learned how they sound on
average, but it doesn't know how you sound. The result is readable
and unsigned. Other lessons on this site point back here when they
talk about the polished AI tone, the smooth finish typical of
generated text.

The problem starts when you copy its versions without working
them. For a week it doesn't show; after a month your emails all
start with *"Thank you for…"* and close with *"Please let me know
if…"*, and look like they came from the same mold. Your readers
can't name it, but they can feel it.

<Collapsible title="An example: the same email before and after">

Your quick draft, before running it through the AI:

*"Hi Marco, the offer's interesting but the timing doesn't work.
I've got too much on my plate until mid-June. If you're up for
it, let's pick this up in July?"*

The version coming out when you ask it to "improve" the text
without specific instructions:

*"Hi Marco, thank you for presenting me with this interesting
opportunity. Unfortunately, at the moment my schedule is
particularly full until mid-June and I'm unable to dedicate to
this project the attention it would deserve. I remain available
to pick up the conversation from July onward, if that works for
you."*

The second is longer, more formal, and says exactly what the
first says. The first is more yours: clear, calibrated to your
relationship with Marco, no padding. If you make this swap ten
times a day for a month, the register of the second becomes your
standard.

</Collapsible>

Two tests before sending: read it aloud, and if it sounds like a
call center reply, cut. Then try to say in a few words what you
mean: if the summary is short and your written version is three
times longer, there's padding. The most common kinds are long
connectives (*"unfortunately at the moment"*), redundant courtesy
formulas, and filler adverbs (*"particularly"*, *"actually"*).

:::tip[Two moves against the average voice]
**At the start of the chat**, if you'll use the AI for writing
more than once, give it a standing instruction. It visibly shifts
the starting register, even though the default doesn't disappear
entirely.

**At the end of the email**, ask yourself: if I had to justify
every single sentence to the recipient as mine, could I? The
passages you couldn't explain in your own words are the ones the
AI put there and you're signing. Rewrite them.
:::

## When to use it, when not to

Most emails live in the middle, and calibration is case by case.
The map below is for tuning yourself, not for pigeonholing.

:::caution[If the content is sensitive, anonymize first]
The three patterns ask you to paste the text, often whole. If the
email is about a third party, a client, a confidential problem,
replace names, companies and identifying data with generic ones
(*"the coworker"*, *"client X"*) before pasting. The picture of
what you share and what you don't is in
[*What you share when you use AI*](/en/fundamentals/privacy-basics/).
:::

**Freely**: short coordination emails (confirmations, follow-ups,
updates), internal documents where clarity matters more than voice
(memos, minutes, operating instructions), technical texts where the
content does all the work. Here the average voice is fine and the
time saved is real.

**With caution**, rereading twice and putting your own hand back
in: texts where you state a position of your own (reviews, open
letters, posts under your name), delicate emails (refusals,
negotiations, criticism) and messages to people who know how you
usually write. The risk is padding, not using the AI itself: an
email with the polished AI tone, from someone who usually writes
dry, sounds like a different person.

If you work in HR, customer success, sales or mediation, many daily
emails fall in this category. Keep pattern 1 (the diagnosis) as
your main habit and write the last 20-30 yards yourself.

**Not from scratch**: messages to people close to you (friends,
family, partner), intimate texts (cards, wishes, private letters),
communications where sincerity is the content (apologies,
condolences, personal statements). The recipient often notices,
even without being able to say why. The AI can help you reread,
soften a harsh passage, check the tone (pattern 3 in miniature),
but you supply the words.
