Differentiating by level and including are two different things,
and confusing them does harm.
[The previous lesson](/en/for-teachers/differentiated-materials/)
was about levels: same topic, different difficulty. Inclusion is a
different axis. A student with a specific learning difficulty can
be among the brightest in the class: they don't need an easier
version, they need to be able to read without the page scrambling
in front of them. The need is accessibility, not a discount on the
content.

Two terms recur in this lesson. **SEN** (special educational
needs) is the broad umbrella: it covers specific learning
difficulties (dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia) but also
language, social, or temporary disadvantages. Each student with
needs has a written support plan: an IEP, a 504 plan, or whatever
your system calls it. Those documents already say what the student
needs. The AI works within those constraints, it doesn't rewrite
them.

## Three axes of adaptation

Adapting a material for accessibility moves along three axes.
Often you combine two or three on the same sheet.

**The shape of the page.** For a student with dyslexia, how the
text is laid out matters as much as what it says. High-legibility
fonts (Atkinson Hyperlegible, Easy Reading, OpenDyslexic), wide
line spacing, generous spacing between words, short lines, text
aligned left and never justified: justification opens "rivers" of
white space that make you lose your line. You set the font and the
spacing yourself in your word processor, not the AI in the chat.
What the AI does is rewrite the text so it fits this format
without getting poorer: it splits long sentences, breaks lines
where it makes sense, drops the asides that stretch the line.

**From paragraph to diagram.** For dyslexia, ADHD, and several
attention difficulties a concept map or a block diagram is often
more readable than ten lines of linear prose. The AI is good at
proposing the structure: you give it the chapter's themes and
their relationships, and it gives you back the skeleton of a
diagram, usually as an indented list or ASCII, not as a finished
image. You validate it and draw it as a real map, or have the
student draw it, which is an exercise in itself. The AI proposes
the skeleton, not the last word: a wrong map confuses more than a
paragraph does, and an AI-generated image often comes out with the
labels in the wrong spots.

**Shorter sentences, same terms.** This is the axis where the AI
goes wrong most often, and it goes wrong in the direction that
looks kind. You ask it for a "simpler" version and, to simplify,
it throws out the technical terms: "sonnet" becomes "poem",
"enjambment" disappears. But the student has to know those terms
at the assessment, the same as their classmates. The right
simplification touches the syntax (short sentences, fewer
subordinate clauses, direct subject-verb-object order) and keeps
the key vocabulary, glossing it if anything: "the sonnet (a
14-line poem)". The constraint to put in the prompt is explicit:
"shorter sentences, but keep all the technical terms and explain
them in parentheses the first time".

:::note[Beyond dyslexia: dyscalculia, dysgraphia, ADHD]
The three axes start from reading, but they adapt. For
**dyscalculia** what counts is the shape of the numbers and the
steps: more worked examples, one operation per line, a number
line, no compressed notation. For **dysgraphia** the weight is on
output: give structures to fill in instead of a blank page, allow
typing instead of handwriting. For **ADHD** and attention
difficulties chunking is the move: one task per screen, short
numbered instructions, less text at a time. The prompt changes,
the method doesn't: adapt the form, keep the content.
:::

## The difference in practice

English literature, year 2 of high school, a textbook paragraph on
Shakespeare's *Sonnets*. There's a student with dyslexia in the
class. The teacher pastes the standard paragraph and asks for the
accessible version.

<ChatDemo
  user={`Adapt this textbook paragraph for a student with dyslexia, year 2 of high school. Shorter sentences, direct order, keep the technical terms and gloss them in parentheses on first use. Add a theme map at the end. Don't cut content.

"Shakespeare's Sonnets, a sequence of 154 poems wrought over an uncertain span and circulated in manuscript before their 1609 printing, trace an intricate emotional triangle among the poet, a fair youth urged toward procreation and poetic immortality, and a dark lady of vexed and sensual attachment."`}
  ai={`**Accessible version**

Shakespeare's Sonnets are a set of 154 poems (a sonnet is a 14-line poem). They were shared in handwritten copies first, then printed in 1609.

The poems are about three people: the poet, a young man (the "fair youth"), and a woman (the "dark lady"). The poet urges the young man to have children and to live on through poetry. The dark lady stands for a difficult, sensual kind of love.

**Theme map**
- Work: the Sonnets -> 154 poems, printed in 1609
- Fair youth -> urged toward children and poetic immortality
- Dark lady -> difficult, sensual attachment`}
/>

The content is all there. The single textbook sentence, four lines
long and full of subordinate clauses, has become a handful of
short sentences in direct order. The terms stayed (*sonnet*, *fair
youth*, *dark lady*) with a gloss in parentheses on first use. The
theme map gives the student the visual skeleton to hang the
details on. What didn't change: the number of poems, the 1609
date, the three figures. Those are the facts the assessment will
ask the whole class.

On a literary text, though, simplifying can flatten a nuance:
"wrought" isn't quite "made", "vexed and sensual attachment" says
more than "a difficult kind of love". Reread the accessible
version against the original and, where the nuance matters for your
subject, put it back by hand with a gloss rather than letting it
drop.

Before you use this version, it also has to go to whoever supports
the student. The chat gave you a quick draft, not a validated
material.

:::caution[AI doesn't replace the SEN coordinator]
The person who knows the student is the SEN coordinator or the
learning-support teacher: they know what's in the support plan,
which strategies have already been agreed with the family, what
worked and what didn't last year. The AI saves you the time of the
rewrite, not the pedagogical judgment. Always pass the adapted
version to whoever supports the student before taking it to class.
:::

:::tip[If the formal network is missing]
Not every school has a SEN coordinator or a learning-support
teacher on hand, and the plan isn't always ready. If the
validating figure isn't there, lean on what exists (the support
plan if there is one, a colleague who had the student, what the
family tells you works at home) and stay more conservative: a
cautious adaptation beats a reckless one. If the plan doesn't
exist yet (an unconfirmed need, an assessment in progress), you
can still make a material more accessible, but the concern goes
through the school's proper channels: the AI doesn't certify and
you don't diagnose.
:::

:::tip[Adapting material you already have]
Often you don't start from scratch: you have a standard sheet
ready and have to adapt it fast. Photograph or scan the page,
upload it to the AI (see
[Photos, images, and files](/en/everyday-use/images-and-files/))
and ask: "rewrite this sheet keeping the same objectives and the
same key terms, with short sentences and a gloss of the hard
terms". It's faster than retyping it all. Check that the AI read
the text from the photo correctly: on crooked scans or difficult
handwriting it sometimes gets a word wrong.
:::

:::caution[Privacy: the student has no name in the chat]
When you describe the case to the AI, no name, no details of the
diagnosis, no family situation. Write "a student with dyslexia",
not "Marco in year 9, class B, diagnosed in 2024". The tool
doesn't need the identity to adapt a text, and the data of a minor
with a formal assessment is among the most sensitive. The
handling of minors' data comes back in detail in the [last lesson
of the module](/en/for-teachers/ethics-of-teaching/).
:::

## What NOT to do

**Don't ask the AI to "assess the severity" of the difficulty.** It
isn't a diagnostic tool and it hasn't seen the student. Questions
like "how severe is this dyslexia" or "what level of support is
needed" get invented answers that sound competent. Diagnosis and
the plan are made by specialists, and they're already written in
the support plan.

**Don't override the plan that already exists.** If the plan says
"maps yes, audio material no" and you have the AI generate an audio
version because it seems like a good idea, you're contradicting
strategies decided with the family and the specialists. The AI
adapts within the plan's constraints, not in place of the plan.

**Don't put labels on the material.** The sheet the student
receives isn't called "the SEN version" and doesn't carry a
different badge. It has to be accessible without being marked. The
same logic as the neutral labels in the lesson on levels applies:
adapting is there to include, not to flag. Often an accessible
version (short sentences, a diagram, glosses) is clearer for
everyone, and nothing stops you from giving it to the whole class:
if everyone gets it, it flags no one.
