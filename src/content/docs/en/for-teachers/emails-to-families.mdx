A wrong word in an email to a parent can cost a meeting that solves
nothing, an escalation to the head's office, in some cases a formal
complaint. But the tone, usually, you can handle: you've done it
for years. The real problem is time. Three delicate emails in a
week, written and rethought word by word, are two hours you don't
have. The AI is for this: compressing the writing time, not
replacing the sensitivity with which you choose what to say and
what not to. The sensitivity stays yours, the AI gives you drafts
to work it on faster.

## Three registers, by school level

The same news is written differently depending on who receives it
and how old the student you're talking about is.

**Primary.** Warm and close in tone. The child is between six and
ten, and the stumbles are stumbles of growing up: the email says so
without dramatizing. A warm opening ("Dear [parent], I wanted to
update you on something I've noticed in class") prepares better than
a bureaucratic one.

**Secondary** (middle and high school). Institutional but not cold
in tone. Here the student is an implicit recipient: the email is
read at home, often in front of them. Writing as if they didn't
exist, or as if they were only a problem to manage, shows. ("Dear
[parent], I'm writing to share an observation about [name]…").

**University.** Formal in tone. Here something legal changes, not
just style: the student is almost always an adult, and
communicating with the family can be inappropriate or forbidden
without their consent. Before writing to the parent of an adult
student, check that it's a legitimate communication and, if needed,
authorized by the student.

## The three-step pattern

The skeleton is the same, whatever the register.

**1. Tell the AI the full situation.** What happened, what you
observed, what you want the family to know or do, and the goal of
the email: to inform, to call a meeting, to ask for cooperation, to
prepare a handover to learning support. The clearer the context,
the less generic the draft. With one absolute constraint: no names
and no sensitive data in the chat (the details are in the note
*Privacy of minors*).

**2. Ask for three versions in different registers.** One drier,
one warmer, one more formal. Not to pick "the right one", but to
have material to draw from. Often the good email is an opening taken
from the warm version, a body from the dry one, a closing from the
formal one.

**3. Compose your own from the pieces and reread it cold.** You
assemble the version that sounds like you, then let it settle. For
delicate emails the rule is to write them in the evening and reread
(and send) them the next morning: cold, you spot the line that
sounds accusatory, the adjective too many, the door closed where
you meant to leave it open. As you reread, also check the AI hasn't
added a detail you didn't give it: it sometimes fills gaps with
plausible, invented particulars, and in an email to a family an
invented fact is trouble.

## The difference in practice

Middle school: a student who was doing well has dropped to failing
in several subjects within a month, and is more withdrawn in class.
The teacher wants to inform the family and propose a meeting.

Context: middle school, a student who until a month ago was doing very well has dropped to failing in several subjects, and is more withdrawn in class than usual. I want to inform the family and propose a meeting, without alarming them and without labels. No names: use "[name]" and "[parent]". Give me three versions: a dry one, a warm one, a formal one.`}
  ai={`**Dry version**
Dear [parent], I'm writing with an update on [name]. Over the past month I've noticed a drop in performance across several subjects and a more withdrawn attitude than before. I'd like to talk it over with you: could you suggest two or three times when you'd be free for a meeting? Best regards.

**Warm version**
Dear [parent], I'm writing because I care about [name] and I've noticed a change that's on my mind. Lately the results have dropped and I see [name] quieter in class, when usually they take part gladly. Often behind these changes there's something simple we can understand together. Would you be up for meeting in the next few days?

**Formal version**
Dear [parent], I am writing to report a decline in [name]'s academic performance over the past month, observed across several subjects and accompanied by reduced participation. I believe a meeting would be appropriate to share our respective observations. I remain available to arrange an appointment.`}
/>

The email the teacher sends isn't any of the three whole. It takes
the opening of the warm one ("I care about [name]"), the factual
body of the dry one (the drop, the attitude, without strong
adjectives), and the closing of the formal one, open to a meeting,
with three proposed dates instead of the vague "whenever you like".
The three versions are there to compose from, not to choose between.

:::caution[Privacy of minors]
Even if you know everything about the student, the chat must not.
In the prompt: no name of the child (write "[name]"), no names of
classmates involved ("argued with [name]" becomes "argued with a
classmate"), no health data ("is asthmatic", "takes a medication"),
no family details ("the parents are separated", "the father works
abroad"). Those details serve your judgment, not the draft. A
minor's data is among the most sensitive, and a consumer
chat isn't the place to enter it for convenience.
:::

## Cases with specific traps

**Low grade.** No emotional words ("worrying", "serious", "grave"):
describe the fact and open to a meeting. The parent supplies the
emotion when they read it.

**Behavior.** No character labels ("is aggressive", "is lazy", "is
rude"): they describe the person, and a parent defends them.
Describe the episodes ("on Tuesday they interrupted the lesson
three times"), which you can look at together without anyone
getting defensive.

**Suspected learning difficulty.** The email isn't the place to say
it, and the AI isn't the tool to hypothesize it. Never write a
diagnosis in an email. The email opens only to an in-person
meeting, where whoever supports the student will also be present.

**Meeting request.** No "as soon as possible" or "at your earliest
convenience" without dates: propose two or three days and concrete
time slots. A
vague request creates anxiety and fixes nothing.

:::tip[If the family doesn't speak English well]
In a class with families who don't speak the language of the school
well, the email risks not being understood, and the problem is no
longer register but comprehensibility. The AI can translate it into
the family's language or rewrite it in simpler English (short
sentences, no bureaucratese). Two cautions: on delicate topics an
automatic translation can lose a nuance or sound blunt, so have it
reread by a colleague or a cultural mediator if the school has one;
and send the original version too, so what you actually wrote stays
on record.
:::

:::tip[Replying to an angry parent]
The hardest email often isn't the one you write, it's the reply to
a parent who complains, sometimes aggressively. The same pattern
holds, with two adjustments. The goal is to
lower the temperature and bring it back to the facts, not to be right: ask the AI
for a factual, non-defensive version that acknowledges the parent's
point without admitting fault that isn't there. And here the
"reread cold" isn't optional: in the heat you reply from the gut,
and one defensive line too many turns a complaint into a case. If
the aggression goes over the line, the email isn't enough: take it
to an in-person meeting, better not alone.
:::

:::tip[When the email isn't the tool]
For serious matters (violence, incidents of a sexual nature,
suspected abuse at home, self-harm) the email isn't enough, and the
AI has nothing to do with it. There's an internal procedure
involving the head, the safeguarding lead, possibly social
services. You follow that, you don't improvise an email to the
parent. The AI is for routine communications: on the serious, the
procedure comes before everything.
:::

## What NOT to do

**Don't send the email without rereading it cold.** Haste produces
many of the emails people regret. A night's distance costs
nothing.

**Don't leave the names in the chat "just to have it written
better".** There's no convenience that justifies putting a minor's
data into an external tool. Anonymize first, always.

**Don't cite the AI as a source of authority.** An "the AI suggests
that…" in a communication to a family is an institutional own goal.
The email is yours, you sign it, the responsibility is yours.
