In class you have twenty people in front of you, starting from
different points: some have met a similar idea before, some know
the words but not the concept, some have never heard of the topic.
The standard explanation, the one the textbook offers, is a
single representation of the idea, and for part of the class it
isn't enough. You need alternative versions ready *before* class,
and one in reserve *during* class for when you see someone slip.

AI does this work in a few minutes, if you know what to ask for.

## The five versions

The five are five representations of the same idea: an example, a
definition, a diagram, a story, an action. They aren't five levels
of difficulty. Seeing a concept through several representations
makes it richer and easier to use in new situations, for every
student. The idea of matching each student to their own "learning
style" (visual, auditory and so on), on the other hand, isn't
supported by research. A stuck student needs another
representation, not to find the one that's "right for them".

1. **Everyday analogy**: ties the concept to an object, a
   situation, a gesture everyone knows. Electric current as water
   in a pipe, entropy as a room that messes itself up, sentence
   syntax as recipe syntax. It gives the idea a concrete example
   to rest on.
2. **Technical register**: the formal definition, with the right
   terminology, the logical structure explicit. It's what the
   exam asks for and what the teacher expects to hear at the
   assessment. It fixes the precise words and the logical order.
3. **Visual mode**: diagram, schema, concept map, timeline. It
   shows the structure of the idea instead of telling it, and
   next to the words it helps students remember it better. Often
   the most undervalued mode by teachers who teach mostly "by
   voice".
4. **Historical narrative**: how we got here. Who understood it
   first, in what order, why it mattered, what changed after. It
   gives the idea a plot and a reason to exist, which abstract
   definitions don't.
5. **Small hands-on activity**: a mini-experiment, a simulation,
   a physical manipulation, an interactive quiz. Typical duration
   between 10 and 20 minutes, needs to be planned in the lesson
   outline. The student does something and extracts the concept
   from what they did.

Visual mode and the hands-on activity overlap but don't coincide:
the visual is **reception** (the student looks at the schema and
rebuilds the structure), the hands-on is **action** (the student
does something and from the doing extracts the concept). A water
cycle diagram projected on the board is visual; students
evaporating water on a hot plate is hands-on.

The pattern is to ask the AI for **all five at once**, on the
same concept, in one turn. One at a time gives the illusion of
more quality but costs twice the time and loses the *comparison*:
the value is in seeing the five side by side and getting in one
look which one opens the class and which one doesn't. Then you
read them cold and pick the two or three that suit the concept
and the class in front of you.

To choose, ask yourself for each version "does this class already
have the vocabulary to grab onto it?". The everyday analogy works
if students know *the analogy* (a farming analogy on an inner-city
class doesn't land). The technical register works if you've already
introduced the discipline's vocabulary. The historical narrative
falls flat without 5 minutes of slack. The visual mode is almost
always doable; the hands-on costs 10-20 minutes of class time.

> *"For a class of 16-17 year-olds in mathematics I need to
> introduce the concept of inverse function. Generate me five
> versions of the same explanation, one per register: everyday
> analogy, technical, visual (describe the schema in words, I'll
> draw it on the board), historical narrative, hands-on activity.
> Each version max 80 words. Don't pick for me, I'll pick later."*

The word limit matters because the AI tends to write mini-essays
and you can't compare the versions at a glance. "Don't pick for me"
stops it from closing with "I'd recommend version 2 because…": that
call is yours, since you know the class.

## The difference in practice

Math teacher, 16-17 year-olds, introducing inverse functions.
Five versions in one turn.

Generate me five versions of the same explanation, one per register: everyday analogy, technical, visual (describe the schema in words), historical narrative, hands-on activity. Each version max 80 words.`}
  ai={`**1. Everyday analogy**
Think of the "undo" button on your phone. If action X takes you from state A to state B, the action that takes you back from B to A is its inverse. Opening a file has closing it as inverse, tying a shoe has untying it. Not all actions have a clean inverse: "breaking a vase" doesn't. Math functions are like these actions: some can be undone by another function, others can't.

**2. Technical register**
Given a function *f*: A → B, its inverse *f⁻¹*: B → A exists if and only if *f* is bijective (injective and surjective). The condition holds: *f⁻¹(f(x)) = x* for every x in A, and *f(f⁻¹(y)) = y* for every y in B. Graphically, the inverse is the reflection of *f* across the line *y = x*. To build it algebraically: write *y = f(x)*, swap *x* and *y*, solve for *y*.

**3. Visual mode**
Draw axes. Plot any function, say *f(x) = 2x + 1*, a line crossing both axes. Then draw the line *y = x* (45° diagonal, dashed). The inverse function is the "ghost" of *f* reflected across that diagonal: every point (a, b) on *f* becomes (b, a) on the inverse. If you fold the paper along the diagonal, the two overlap.

**4. Historical narrative**
The concept matures in the 17th century with Newton and Leibniz, but as "an operation that undoes". Newton works on fluxions (integration as the inverse of differentiation). Only in the 19th century, with Cauchy and then Dirichlet, do we reach the modern definition of function and from there a clean concept of inverse. The question "is a function invertible?" becomes central: it isn't enough to write it, you have to check that it "goes back".

**5. Small hands-on activity**
Hand out pairs of numbers (x, y) from a simple function, say f(x) = x + 3 → (1,4), (2,5), (3,6). In pairs, students have to find the rule that takes you from y to x. Then you ask: "write the function that does this work". To wrap up, try f(x) = x² applied to (-2, 4) and (2, 4): what happens? The inverse there doesn't work cleanly. Why?`}
/>

</Collapsible>

Five versions on the table. The teacher's call, based on the
concept and the class: in a class strong in algebra but light on geometric
intuition, versions 2 and 3 do the work; in a less technical but
curious class, 1 and 5 open the lesson and 2 closes at the end.
Version 4 (historical) is kept as a small five-minute aside, to
use if the class responds well to the first two.

The typical mistake is using *all five* in the same lesson: you
end up with confusion. There are five versions to give you a
choice. Two or three done well help more than five rushed.

<Collapsible title="It also works for subjects without formulas">

The example above is mathematical but the pattern holds in
literature, philosophy, art history, languages. Version 2
"technical register" translates to the critical vocabulary of the
discipline: for a Shakespeare sonnet it's meter and rhetorical
figures; for a painting it's composition, perspective, light.
Version 4 "historical narrative" is almost always in the textbook
already, and the AI reframes it to fit your class. Versions 1, 3,
5 work identically.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Visual mode without drawing skills">

If the visual mode calls for a diagram and you don't have time or
a steady hand, you have two options. The first: ask the AI to
describe it in words (as it does in step 3 of the ChatDemo on
inverse functions), then copy it on the board in 30 seconds
(almost always doable). The second: generate a simple ASCII
version in chat and project it, no drawing needed. Don't ask the
AI to generate a real image with complex graphics: it often comes out looking right but with errors (wrong axes,
labels in the wrong spots, invented names). On technical topics,
the teacher's board is still more reliable.

</Collapsible>

## On the fly: when a student doesn't click

You've explained the concept with the version you picked. Three
signals tell you whether it's working before the formative check:
the **eyes** (the student looking elsewhere, looking out the
window, looking at the desk instead of the board is one who's
slipped), **quick application questions** (ask "give me your own
example of X" to two students at random: if they stumble, the
version isn't landing), **hands going up on their own** (zero
hands in a class that's usually responsive often means
embarrassment, not understanding). If the signals are negative,
you need **another representation, now**, not a quarter-hour of
rework. The student doesn't have the wrong style: the version you
used wasn't enough, and they need another one.

Working pattern, in 60 seconds:

1. Keep a tablet or phone on the desk with an AI chat already
   open. If you always use the same tool, a pre-built "teacher
   project" with standard instructions (Claude and ChatGPT
   Projects, Custom GPTs, Gemini Gems or skills: the names
   change) saves the first 30 seconds.
2. Type fast: "concept X, class didn't grab on with the
   [technical/visual/etc.] version. Give me another angle in 3
   sentences, using a [analogical/visual/narrative] representation".
3. Read what comes out, *translate it into your own voice* (don't
   read it word for word), and turn back to the board or to the
   students.

<Collapsible title="What to put in a teacher project">

A project or assistant set up in advance saves you the first 30
seconds. Useful instructions to put in it:
your subject, your typical school level, the principle "answer in
max 3 sentences, classroom spoken register, no bullets, no
preambles, one angle per turn". A "class vocabulary" file (the
terms you've already introduced) helps calibration. The smaller
and more focused the project, the faster and more on target it
answers.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="If you can't pull out a phone in class">

Some schools ban phones and tablets for teachers in class too, or
the connection is poor. In those cases the pure on-the-fly isn't
possible, but you have an alternative: prep **two or three
alternative angles ahead of time** (jotted at the end of the
outline), not just the main version. If the first doesn't land,
the second is already in your memory, not on the phone. AI helps
in prep, not in "instant rescue".

</Collapsible>

The class never sees these three steps: as far as they can tell,
*you* had "another idea". Step 3 matters most: the voice, the
rhythm and the eye contact with the class are yours. Reading the
chat word for word breaks the lesson and sounds fake.

## "By representation" is different from "by level"

Five versions of the same explanation are five **ways of looking
at the same idea**. They aren't five levels of difficulty. All
the students, after seeing it from several sides, have understood
the same concept, at the level the curriculum sets.

Differentiation **by level** is something else: the same
students, on the same topic, do things **of different difficulty**
(consolidation / standard / extension). That's the next lesson
of the module, [*Differentiated materials by
level*](/en/for-teachers/differentiated-materials/).

The two techniques combine in real classes: first you think about
the **level**, then for each one you pick the **representations**
that land. The useful combinations are usually three or four, not
the fifteen possible.

## What to avoid

**Bringing all five versions to the same class in the same
lesson.** More representations help, but a lesson that runs
through five angles on the same idea in 60 minutes stays at a
surface level on each one.

**Using the historical narrative without verifying the
data.** AI invents dates, attributions, chronological orders
quite easily ("X figured it out first, in 1832, after a
conversation with Y"). If your historical narrative sits on a
fake fact, the anecdote becomes a classroom myth your students
will carry with them. Three quick checks that fit in 10 minutes
before the bell: search Wikipedia for the name and the year (if
the intro doesn't mention them, that's a first signal); ask *a
second AI* (ChatGPT vs Claude vs Gemini) the same thing, and if
the dates change you know it's invented; keep the anecdote vague
if you aren't sure ("in the 19th century, some German
mathematicians…") rather than inventing a specific name.

**Reading out loud in class what the AI wrote.** This holds for
prep and even more for on-the-fly: rework it in your own voice.

Five versions give the whole class more ways to grasp the same
concept. But the class isn't uniform on *level* either: some students are
ahead and some are behind on the same topic. The
