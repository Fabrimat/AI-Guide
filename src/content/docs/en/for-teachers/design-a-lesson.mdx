This module helps you use AI as a prep colleague (outline,
materials, assessment questions, sensitive emails) and as a critic:
a second pair of eyes on what you've already written. It doesn't
know who your class is, how it reacts to a question, when group
work flies and when it falls apart. That part is yours. You walk
into class, with your voice. Pulling out your phone mid-lesson to
ask for another angle for a student who didn't get it can make
sense: the next lesson covers it, and it's a tactical use, not a
hand-off.

:::tip[The first time costs more]
The first two or three lessons you design with this method take
longer than doing them by hand. From the third onwards it flips:
the outline comes out in 20 minutes instead of an hour, and the
materials are already polished. The upfront cost is learning the
pattern.
:::

## The three-stage outline

For a single lesson of 50 to 90 minutes, ask the AI for three
things in sequence, not one blob "make me a lesson on X". The
sequence mirrors how you think about teaching when you're not in a
rush: first what, then how, then timings.

### 1. Learning objective

**One main objective per lesson**, with an active verb: describe,
apply, distinguish, build, compare. One or two secondary objectives
can come along, but the central one is the only one you check at
the end and build the outline on. Three equally central objectives
in 60 minutes signal an overloaded lesson, not an ambitious one.
"Know" and "learn" are too vague to measure whether the lesson
worked.

> *"For a science lesson with a class of around 12-year-olds (60
> minutes), help me write a single learning objective on the
> water cycle. Use an active verb from <Def explanation="A six-level scale of cognitive skills: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create. Useful for calibrating the difficulty of questions and activities.">Bloom's taxonomy</Def>. Give me three
> alternatives with increasing difficulty, so I can choose."*

Three alternatives help calibration. The simplest ("describe the
three stages of the cycle") only asks for recall, the middle one
("explain the role of the sun and gravity") adds causality, the
hardest ("predict what changes in a cycle with less heat") asks for
application. If none convinces you, the AI probably hasn't picked
up the class constraint: reload with more detail ("this class has
already covered X, struggles with Y, has 50 effective minutes").
If all three feel plausible, pick the one whose success you can
*observe* in five minutes of formative check at the end.

### 2. Sequencing

Once you've picked the objective, ask for the structure of the
lesson in stages. Four typical stages, with flexibility:

> *"Consider the objective I chose. Propose a four-stage
> structure: activating opener, discovery or explanation,
> guided application, synthesis. For each stage: what happens
> and what the class does (not what the teacher does). No
> timings yet."*

The constraint "what the class does" matters. AI tends to write
plans from the teacher's point of view ("explain the concept of
evaporation") when what makes a lesson work is what the class is
doing ("students observe a glass of water in the sun and try to
explain what's happening").

### 3. Realistic timings

Only now do you ask for timings. Ask for structure and timings
together and the AI crams activities into the available time
instead of calibrating time to the activities you validated: the
outline looks fine on paper, but every stage is squeezed 30% tighter
than it needs.

> *"Based on the validated structure, give me a duration estimate
> for each stage, 60 minutes total. The class has 24 students,
> mixed ability, and you should factor in 5 minutes of classroom
> management at the start and another 5 at the end."*

The AI's output here is always optimistic. Three 15-minute
activities in 50 minutes look reasonable, but in real life you walk
in late, the first question takes twice as long, two students ask to
leave at the same moment, and ten minutes before the bell you
haven't reached the synthesis.

:::tip[The minus 25-30% rule]
Sum the minutes the AI proposes and subtract 25 to 30 percent.
That's the margin for classroom management, transitions, unplanned
questions, small tech hiccups. If the AI proposes 65 minutes for a
50-minute lesson, it needs cutting, not compressing.

It's an educated guess, not a lab measurement. For small classes
trained on the method it can drop to 15%, for big or restless ones
it rises to 40%. Calibrate to yours after two or three lessons.
:::

<Collapsible title="Lessons outside standard durations">

The four-stage pattern works up to 90-100 minutes. Beyond that
(triple period, lab, intensive module), don't stretch the stages:
replicate the cycle. A 3-hour session is two or three full cycles
plus a final synthesis that ties it all together. For co-taught
sessions, ask the AI who leads each stage before asking for
timings.

</Collapsible>

## Critique the outline before the materials

You have a reasonable outline. Don't write exercises, slides or a
summary handout yet: send the whole outline back to the AI and ask
for a critique.

> *"This is the outline I've built for a science lesson, class of
> 12-year-olds, water cycle, 60 minutes. Tear it apart: what's
> missing, what's too much, where the class might lose focus,
> which stage risks running over. No compliments, be direct."*

Without "tear it apart", AI replies with a string of "great
structure, well laid out": not much use. "No compliments" unblocks
the critical judgment. Typical findings: the explanation stage is
dense and the class loses focus mid-way, the opening example is too
abstract for the age group, there's no intermediate formative
check, the synthesis is just review.

Not every critique deserves to be taken on. Keep first the ones you
recognize as real problems for your class: "dense explanation" is
true if you know this class can't hold 15 minutes of teacher talk,
and isn't with a motivated senior group. Among the real ones, keep
the top two or three, because working on all of them stalls you.
Then rewrite the outline in those spots, and *only then* move on to
the materials.

## Materials anchored to the validated outline

After the critique you generate materials one at a time, pasting
the validated outline into the prompt each time.

<PromptCompare
  weak="Write the opening exercise for the water cycle lesson."
  strong={`I'm pasting the validated outline below. Write the opening exercise.
Duration: 8 minutes. Mode: individual work.
Expected output from the class: two written lines in the notebook.
Consistent with the objective "explain the role of the sun in the water's change of state".
Also add: what I should expect as student answers, and a classroom-management note.

[validated outline]`}
  note="The second prompt gives duration, mode, output and objective, and asks for the two things the AI won't offer on its own: expected answers and classroom management."
/>

Same pattern for the application handout, the synthesis slide, the
mini formative check. That level of detail only comes out if you
ask for it, and it's what makes a material usable 15 minutes before
the bell.

There's a **stop rule**: if while generating materials you notice
the outline needs to change (the opening can't fill 5 minutes, the
discovery stage needs materials you hadn't planned), go back to the
outline and don't patch the material. Three materials consistent
with a corrected outline beat five consistent with a broken one.

## The difference in practice

Science teacher, class of 12-year-olds, introductory lesson on the
water cycle, 60 minutes. First try, no constraints.

  ai={`Here's a proposed lesson:

**Opening (10 min)**: introduction to the water cycle using a short video animation. Class discussion of students' prior knowledge.

**Explanation (25 min)**: presentation of the four phases (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection). For each phase: definition, example, illustration.

**Activity (15 min)**: students in groups draw a diagram of the water cycle.

On paper it works, but a closer look shows the problems.
Twenty-five minutes of frontal explanation to 12-year-olds is too
much. The group activity is generic and doesn't force students to
use the objective. The synthesis is review only. And the 60 minutes
add up because there's no room for classroom management or
transitions.

- Guided discovery (15 min): three pairs at random present their chosen explanation. The teacher gathers hypotheses on the board, introduces the technical terms (evaporation, condensation) anchoring them to the students' guesses.
- Intermediate formative check (5 min): quick three-question quiz (paper or digital) to see who has grasped the link between heat and change of state.
- Application (15 min): the class extends the model to the full cycle, working in groups of four on a worksheet asking to draw and annotate the cycle for a puddle in the sun, from start to end.

*Material*: a transparent glass with two fingers of water, covered by a small plate. It's been sitting on the desk for 30-40 minutes, in the sun coming through the window.

*Instruction for students* (write on the board or project):
"Look closely at the covered glass. Without leaving your seat, in two lines on your notebook: 1) what you see different inside the glass compared to before; 2) how you try to explain it, even if you don't have a scientific answer."

*Time*: 8 minutes (2 of silent observation, 6 of writing). No help, no correction at this stage.

*What to expect as output from the class*: many will say "there's water on the plate" and try explanations like "it came out of the glass", "it dripped off the plate", "the glass is sweating". The two versions are about the same length, but in the second every
minute is tied to a concrete activity and every activity to the
objective. The material is specific to the actual outline, where
the first version gave you something generic ("they draw a diagram
of the water cycle").

## Your voice in class stays yours

An outline that sounds like a standard teaching plan is a sign
you've delegated too much. Reread and rewrite by hand the spots
that count in front of the class: the opening hook, the example you
narrate, the joke that breaks the rhythm, the question you close
on. The outline is the frame, not the lesson script. If you reread
it and hear a neutral textbook voice instead of yours, small
changes are usually enough: an example of your own, an instruction
in the wording you normally use, a turn of phrase you'd never say
out loud.

<Collapsible title="Multi-day lessons and subjects with other structures">

The pattern scales to a four- or six-lesson unit if you ask the AI
to treat the outline as "lesson 1 of 4 in a unit on X", then
iterate for the next ones. The constraint to set at the start is
what students should be able to do at the end of the whole unit,
not the single lesson. Keep everything in a **single chat**:
context from previous lessons helps the AI avoid repetition. If the
chat gets too long, summarize in a new one what you've decided so
far.

The "activating opener, discovery, application, synthesis"
structure is typical but not universal. Music, P.E., art, labs and
clinical practice use different sequences (for P.E.: warm-up,
technique, application or game). Swap the standard stages for yours
at step 2: the principle "what does the class do at each stage"
stays.

</Collapsible>

## What to avoid

**Asking "make me the lesson" without an outline.** AI produces a
dense blob in a neutral voice. The objective, structure, timings
sequence is what makes the output reusable.

**Pasting students' personal data into the chat.** If during prep
you make examples about a specific student ("Marco struggles with
charts, how do I structure the application stage?"), drop the name.
The privacy of minors is covered in
[*Ethics of teaching*](/en/for-teachers/ethics-of-teaching/).

**Reading out loud in class what the AI wrote.** Its sentences have
a recognizable rhythm even when the content is correct, and in
class that rhythm sounds fake. Rework them in your own voice.
