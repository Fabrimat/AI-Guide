A class isn't uniform. On the same topic, some students arrive
ready and get bored, some are right on track, and some struggle
and fall behind. Give everyone the same sheet and you lose
two-thirds of them: the first because it's too little, the last
because it's too much. The teaching answer is to differentiate by
level: same content, versions of different difficulty.

This is a different axis from the previous lesson.
[Explain in five different ways](/en/for-teachers/explain-in-five-ways/)
works *by style*: the same difficulty, told from different angles.
Here you work *by level*: the same angle, at different depths. You
need it when the class is mixed, when you run catch-up in parallel
with the main group, or when a unit runs over several days and you
have to keep the fast and the slow together.

## One base text, three derivatives

The weak spot of differentiating by hand is time: writing three
sheets from scratch is work few teachers actually do, so you end
up giving everyone the same one. The weak spot of differentiating
with AI, if you use it badly, is the opposite: you ask for three
separate sheets and the AI reinvents the content at each pass, so
the "easy" sheet and the "hard" sheet no longer talk about the
same topic.

The pattern that avoids both problems is this: **write a single
base text**, the one at your class's standard (you write it, or
assemble it from the textbook). Then ask the AI to *derive* two
variants from it, not to write two new ones:

- a **consolidation** version: simpler vocabulary, shorter
  sentences, more concrete examples, exercises that ask students
  to apply rather than analyze;
- an **extension** version: richer vocabulary, more abstract
  examples, exercises that ask students to compare or argue.

The levels in class are three (consolidation, standard,
extension), but the base text is already the standard: you only
ask the AI for the two ends. The base text stays the reference.
The two variants are adaptations of the same content, up and down
from the standard line. That way the three levels stay aligned:
they talk about the same topic, with the same facts, at three
different depths.

The typical prompt is explicit about this:

> *"This is my base text for a first-year high school class. Derive
> two variants of the same content: a consolidation one (shorter
> sentences, simpler vocabulary, application exercises) and an
> extension one (comparison and argumentation exercises). Keep the
> same key terms and the same facts. Don't add new topics."*

## Three rules that keep the levels aligned

Deriving the variants is half the work. The other half is checking
they stay the same lesson at three speeds, not three different
lessons. Three constraints to put in the prompt and to verify
afterward.

**Same answers to the base questions.** The exercises on the three
sheets can differ: the consolidation one asks students to apply,
the extension one to argue. What has to stay identical is the core
of facts and definitions under the exercises, the same core the
final assessment will ask everyone. On the underlying question the
three sheets must allow compatible correct answers: what varies is
the depth the student reaches it at, not the fact. If the student
on sheet A and the one on sheet C give irreconcilable answers to
"what does the Edict of Milan establish", one of the two variants
has changed the content, not just the level.

**Same working time.** The three sheets have to take the same
nominal time. The classic mistake is the extension one ending up
with twice the exercises of the consolidation one: the student
who's ahead is punished with more work, the one who's behind with
less. Ask the AI for the constraint explicitly ("each must be
completable in 25 minutes") and time one sheet yourself to check.

**Same glossary.** The key terms of the base text appear in all
three variants. Students need to be able to use them in oral
discussion and in the assessment that follows, which is the same
for everyone. What changes is *how* they're explained, not *which*
ones are there. A consolidation variant that strips out the
technical terms looks kinder, but it puts those students at a
disadvantage when everyone comes back to the same ground.

## The difference in practice

History, first year of high school, Constantine's reforms. The
teacher pastes their base text, at the class standard, and asks
the AI for the two variants.

The two variants say the same things. The consolidation one lines
them up with short sentences and an application exercise ("match
each reform to the problem"). The extension one ties the reforms
together and asks for an argument ("why Rome wasn't enough
anymore"). The three key terms (Edict of Milan, capitatio,
Constantinople) stay in both: what changes is how much they're
contextualized, not whether they're there. The standard base
text, the one pasted into the prompt, sits in between: plain
sentences and an explanation exercise, without the connections
the extension version asks for.

To catch whether the AI shifted a fact rather than just the level,
reread each variant against the base text, not against your
memory. The base text is the yardstick: if a variant states
something that isn't there, the AI derived it wrong.

:::note[For subjects with problems and exercises]
In math, physics, chemistry the "base text" is a core of concepts
plus a set of problems. The consolidation version doesn't get "more
problems" or "harder problems": that
would break the same-time rule. What changes is the
*support*. The consolidation version keeps the same problem but
with more scaffolding (a worked example, then a near-identical
twin, the steps guided). The extension version removes the
scaffolding and adds transfer (the same method on a new case, or
"explain why this step works"). Same number of problems, same
expected results, different support.
:::

:::tip[For primary school the scheme changes]
With children between 6 and 10 the three variants tend not to
work: the levels are too close to feel distinct, and they end up
confusing. Alternative pattern: base text plus **a single**
consolidation variant (more images, shorter sentences, a hands-on
or movement exercise). Extension for the more advanced children
takes other forms, not a harder sheet: a real-world task, the role
of tutor to a classmate, a small piece of research to report back
to the class.
:::

:::note[Combining by style and by level]
In practice you combine
[style](/en/for-teachers/explain-in-five-ways/) and level. For the
same unit you pick the level first (consolidation, standard,
extension), then for each one the style that lands (analogy,
technical register, visual, historical, activity). No need to
produce fifteen variants by crossing everything: three levels by
five styles is a warehouse you'll never use. The combinations
useful to a real class are usually three or four, picked knowing
your students.
:::

## What NOT to do

**Don't have the variants generated without the base text.** If you ask
"make me an easy sheet and a hard one on Constantine's reforms"
without giving it your text, the AI invents the content from
scratch for each variant, decides for itself what matters, and you
lose control over which facts and which terms end up where. The
base text keeps the derivatives anchored.

**Don't label the sheets "easy / medium / hard".** Students notice
right away, and the "easy" sheet becomes a brand. The one who gets
it feels cornered, the one who sees a classmate with the "hard"
one reads a hierarchy. Use neutral labels: A, B, C, or three
colors, or no visible label at all and a map you keep yourself.
The point is to include, and ranking students in public defeats it.

**Don't let the variants drift apart by assessment time.** If the
final assessment is the same for everyone (and it usually is), a
consolidation variant that has cut the key terms or skipped a fact
leaves those students exposed on test day. Reread the three sheets
side by side before handing them out, with the question: "can a
student who studied A answer the assessment I give to C?".

simpler sheet isn't what's needed, an *accessible* one is. The
