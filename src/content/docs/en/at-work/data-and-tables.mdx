You have an Excel table with the wrong columns, customer
names written fifteen different ways, dates half in European
format and half American, and a formula you can't remember.
Without AI you're in for an hour of copy-paste by hand. With
AI, ten minutes.

The time saved is real, but there's one catch you can't skip:
a work spreadsheet isn't your journal. It has customers,
suppliers, contracts, revenue. Before pasting it into a public
chat, take thirty seconds to decide what leaves the company
and what doesn't.

This lesson is the practical toolkit: three concrete use
cases, a privacy section that's the most important piece, and
a rule for when to stop asking AI and open the right tool.

## Three concrete use cases

The three below cover 80% of what a non-developer knowledge
worker does with a spreadsheet. They're everyday moves, none
of them requires code.

### 1. Cleaning inconsistent data

The most common case. A "Customer" column with entries like
*"Smith John"*, *"J. Smith"*, *"smith j"*, *"John Smith Ltd"*.
Dates written as *"12/03/2026"*, *"2026-03-12"*, *"March 12"*.
Duplicates with different capitalization. By hand it's an
afternoon; with AI it's five minutes.

The prompt works if you give it an extract (ten to twenty
representative rows) plus an explicit normalization rule:

> *I'm pasting ten rows from the "Customer" column of our
> CRM. Normalize them to "First Last" format
> (e.g., "John Smith"). If there's a business suffix like
> "Ltd" or "Inc", put it after a comma. Give me back the
> clean table plus one line with any open doubts.*

The two pieces that make the difference: the rule (*"First
Last"*, *"suffix after the comma"*) and the explicit ask for
doubts. Without that, AI normalizes even where it should ask
you how to read an ambiguous case (*"J. Smith"* is John or
James?).

The same logic applies to dates, with one note on locale: if
you work in European format, state *"DD/MM/YYYY format"* or
*"YYYY-MM-DD format"* explicitly. AI tends to default to the
US format (MM/DD/YYYY), and if your data is mixed it can read
*"03/04/2026"* as *"April"* instead of *"March"*. One line of
constraint fixes the problem.

What if the rows are five hundred, not ten? Two paths. The
first, practical one, is the **sample, rule, extend** pattern:
you pass ten to twenty representative rows, work out the
cleanup rule with AI, then apply it to the rest in Excel with
a formula (*find and replace*, `PROPER`, `MID`) or a macro.

The second is uploading the full CSV as an attachment: Claude
and ChatGPT handle long PDFs and CSVs up to a few thousand
rows well, as long as the file is within the platform's
limits. Between the two, the first is usually safer: it keeps you in
control of how the rule gets applied, and it doesn't dump
five hundred rows of company data into a single AI call.

### 2. Excel or Sheets formulas you can't remember

*"How do I count how many times a value appears in a column?"*
*"How do I sum only the January rows?"* *"How do I compare two
columns and find the differences?"* AI gives you the exact
formula and a one-line explanation.

What to pass here is **which tool you use** and **which
language it's installed in**. Both change the syntax:

- Excel in most European languages uses local function names
  (e.g., Italian `SOMMA.SE`, French `SOMME.SI`).
- Excel in English (and Google Sheets, which is always English
  internally) uses `SUMIF`, `COUNTIF`, `VLOOKUP`.
- The parameter separator is `;` in European locales, `,` in
  English ones.
- The decimal separator is `,` in most of Europe, `.` in
  English.

Say it to AI in the prompt: *"English Excel"* or *"Google
Sheets"* or *"Italian Excel"*. If you don't specify, sometimes
you get a formula you can't paste as-is and have to adapt by
hand.

The real value is the explanation: always ask *"explain what
each part of the formula does"* the first time. Next time
you'll write it yourself or adapt it to a different case.
Without the explanation, you've solved today but learned
nothing.

If you paste the formula and Excel answers with `#NAME?`,
`#VALUE!`, or `#REF!`, don't start from scratch. Copy the
error as-is into the chat and ask *"this is the error I'm
getting, what's wrong?"*. Nine times out of ten it's one of
three things: wrong separator (`,` where it wants `;`),
function in the wrong language (`SUMIF` in a non-English
Excel), or a cell reference that doesn't exist on your sheet.
Pasting the error back into the chat is faster than guessing
the fix.

### 3. Small analyses on extracts

*"On these 50 orders, what's the average value per customer?
How many customers spend above the average? Is there a clear
seasonality?"* AI reads, computes, and answers.

Two precautions. **First**, pass the extract in a form AI
reads well: a pasted CSV, or an attachment (everything from
[*Photos, images, and files*](/en/everyday-use/images-and-files/)
applies). Screenshotted tables work, but a text CSV is more
reliable because AI reads it character by character, with no
risk of mistaking one digit for another in a blurry photo.

**Second**, verify a sample. Ask AI for the calculation and
then re-check a few rows by hand or with an Excel formula. A
handy rule: **5% of the rows, minimum three**. If the dataset
is 50 rows you re-check three; if it's 500, you re-check
twenty-five; if it's five thousand, the use case is probably
wrong, and the section
[*AI for ad-hoc, BI for recurring*](#ai-for-ad-hoc-bi-for-recurring)
covers when to switch to a dedicated tool.

Pick rows that cover three profiles: one in the middle, one
at the extremes (highest or lowest value), one with empty or
oddly formatted fields. Not because AI is often wrong, but
because when it is, it's wrong with the same confidence as
when it's right, and on numbers a digit shifted by one
position is an error you can't catch at a glance. The pattern
is the same as in
[*When to trust it and when not to*](/en/fundamentals/when-to-trust-it/).

## Privacy: the main constraint

A CSV with customer names, amounts, addresses, revenue **is a
company document**. Even if it sits on your laptop and isn't
inside a CRM, even if you're handling it on
your own, it's company data. Before uploading it to a public
chat (ChatGPT Free/Plus, Claude Free/Pro, Gemini standard),
three moves apply, in this order.

1. **Ask whether a policy exists.** In many mid-size and
   large companies, IT or legal has already decided what can
   be uploaded and where. If there's a guideline, follow it
   and save yourself the rest of the thinking. If there isn't
   (or if you're a freelancer and the policy is you), the
   handy test is this: *"would I be fine with this data
   showing up on a newspaper tomorrow?"*. If the answer is
   no, the two moves below aren't optional.
2. **Extract only the columns you need.** If you want to know
   how much customers spent on average, you don't need their
   address, their tax ID, or their phone number. Delete those
   columns from the extract before uploading. Less data
   uploaded, less risk.
3. **Anonymize the names.** Replace *"John Smith"* with
   *"Customer 1"*, *"Beta Inc"* with *"Customer 2"*, and keep
   the mapping table in a separate sheet. When AI returns the
   analysis, you map it back to the real names. It's the same
   move as in
   [*Photos, images, and files*](/en/everyday-use/images-and-files/),
   applied to tabular data.

These three moves don't cover everything. Contracts with
confidentiality clauses, health data, special category data
(GDPR art. 9) are a notch higher: anonymization isn't enough,
you need a dedicated company channel or no public AI at all.
A later lesson in this module, *Company data and privacy*,
covers that level.

:::note[Public AI or company AI?]
If your company has given you a Team or Enterprise account, or
a Microsoft Copilot / ChatGPT Enterprise / Claude Team tenant,
the privacy terms change: the data you upload there typically
isn't used for training and stays inside the company
perimeter. If you use AI for work regularly and have access
to one of these plans, that's the right place for company
data. If instead you open ChatGPT or Claude with your personal
account, you're on the consumer version and the three moves
above apply.

A quick way to tell: the plan name appears in your account
settings. If you see *Plus*, *Pro*, *Free*, it's consumer; if
you see *Team*, *Enterprise*, *Business*, or your company's
logo next to your name, it's business. ChatGPT Plus is still
consumer even though it's paid: the payment unlocks features,
it doesn't change the data perimeter.
:::

## AI for ad-hoc, BI for recurring

AI is a copilot for the one-off case: you have a sheet
tonight, you need to clean it up, you paste and go. If
instead the same analysis comes back every month (a
dashboard, a quarterly report, a weekly KPI for the team), AI
is the wrong tool.

Recurring analysis is what **Business Intelligence** tools
are for (Power BI, Looker, Tableau), or the native dashboard
inside your CRM or ERP. There's a structural reason for this:
once it's set up, the dashboard refreshes itself, it's
reproducible, and it's shareable with the team. With AI
you start from scratch every time, you risk a slightly
different answer than last month, and no one can trace how
you got a number.

Practical rule: if you'll ask the same question again within a
month, ask a tool other than AI. If it's a question
that's born today and dies tonight, AI is fine.

## A concrete example

You have a five-row extract from a *Customer* column in the
CRM, written inconsistently. You want them
normalized to *First Last* format to import into a clean
Excel sheet.

<ChatDemo
  user={`I'm pasting five rows from the "Customer" column. Normalize them to "First Last" format (e.g., "John Smith"). If there's a business suffix, put it after a comma. Give me back the clean table plus one closing line with any open doubts.

- smith j.
- J. Smith
- John Smith Ltd
- SMITH JOHN
- j smith`}
  ai={`Clean table:

1. John Smith
2. J. Smith
3. John Smith, Ltd
4. John Smith
5. J. Smith

**Open doubt:** rows 2 and 5 have only the first initial ("J."). They could be the same John Smith as the other rows, or a different customer with initial J (James, Jack, Jared). If you have access to the original data with customer IDs, verify before treating them as duplicates.`}
/>

What AI got right: uppercase/lowercase normalized, *First
Last* order respected, business suffix separated out. Better
still: it didn't decide on its own that *"J. Smith"* was
*"John Smith"*, it flagged the doubt. The *"plus one line
with open doubts"* constraint in the prompt is what triggered
that behavior.

Without that constraint, AI would have probably written
*"John Smith"* everywhere, merging rows that might have been
different customers, and you wouldn't have noticed.

## What NOT to do

**Don't trust numbers without verification.** On small
analyses, always re-check two or three rows by hand. AI can
miss an average, a rounding, or miscount rows that meet a
condition. The verification pattern is the one from
[*When to trust it and when not to*](/en/fundamentals/when-to-trust-it/),
applied to numbers.

**Don't upload entire datasets of sensitive data.** If you
have a CSV of ten thousand rows with customers and amounts,
you don't need to upload all of it to ask for a formula.
Extract ten representative, anonymized rows, and pass those.
For the same reasons as in
[*Privacy: the main constraint*](#privacy-the-main-constraint).

**Don't use AI as an analyst for important business
decisions.** Financial planning, investment appraisal,
customer analysis for a strategic decision: those need a real
analyst (or you, with AI as a targeted helper), not AI as the
author of the conclusion. The perimeter is the one from
[*Things NOT to do*](/en/everyday-use/what-not-to-do/): where
not to delegate the decision, regardless of how good the
answer might be.
