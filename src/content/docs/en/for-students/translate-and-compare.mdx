Translation is one of those areas where AI "looks perfect" and can still
be subtly wrong. A wrong translation of a line of Catullus, or of an
article from *Le Monde*, isn't visible at a glance: you only see it when
you compare it with the original text, with other translations or with a
dictionary. The fluency of the answer tells you nothing about its
fidelity to the text.

This lesson shows how to use AI as a **help** for translation. It
applies to people studying modern languages, to people doing classics
(Latin, Greek), to comparative literature, to <Def explanation="Rigorous study of ancient texts: reconstructing the original from copies, dating them, attributing them, interpreting their historical lexicon. It's the discipline that says 'this line is by Catullus, and here's the textual problem'.">philology</Def>.
Translation is a learning tool, not a finished object you paste into the
assignment.

## The three-versions pattern

Never ask "translate this sentence for me". Always ask for **three
versions with different angles**. For a sentence of French prose, a
line of verse, a long period:

- **Literal**: as close as possible to the original text, even at
  the cost of sounding unnatural in English. It keeps word order,
  lexical choices, syntactic constructions.
- **Idiomatic**: smooth English, small syntactic liberties to avoid
  betraying the meaning. It's the translation you'd find in a good
  current edition.
- **Free**: the idea comes through, but the phrasing changes. More
  adaptation than translation, useful for seeing where you can
  "break" the text without losing it.

The difference between the three shows you what you "lose" in
translation, and that's the real learning. People who translate seriously
recompose a version of their own by reading the three. People studying a
language use them to surface structures that a single translation hides
(a French participle that becomes a subordinate clause in idiomatic
English, a Latin ablative that unfolds into a whole sentence).

The base prompt, to adapt:

> *"Translate this sentence in three versions: a literal one, an
> idiomatic one, a free one. For each one, explain in one line the
> main choice you made."*

The line of explanation matters as much as the translations: it's where
AI shows you its move. If the three versions come back very similar, the
text is "transparent" between the two languages, and that's information.
If you suspect laziness, ask explicitly *"push the differences, even take
them to extremes"*.

## Glosses and historical notes

For texts that need historical understanding (Latin, Greek, Old Italian,
Old English) a modern translation isn't enough: a word may have a
different historical meaning. *Let* in Shakespeare often means "hinder",
not "permit": *"I'll make a ghost of him that lets me"* in *Hamlet* trips
up a modern reader who only knows the current sense. *Patroni* in a Roman
context isn't the "boss" of modern vocabulary.

The move is to ask AI to **pair the translation with glosses**:
words or concepts a modern reader doesn't recognize, explained in
two lines. Like:

> *"Translate this line of Catullus for me. For the terms *patroni*
> and *mos maiorum*, add a two-line gloss on the historical value in
> the Roman context of the first century BC."*

Important caution: historical glosses are one of the points where AI
invents most readily, with wrong dates, attributions to authors who never
existed, garbled etymologies. Verify at least one gloss out of two with a
serious encyclopedic source, prioritizing the riskiest: proper names,
dates, work titles, etymologies. **Treccani** (the major Italian
encyclopedia, free online) for Italian and historical concepts, **Oxford
Classical Dictionary** for the classics (your library almost certainly
has access). The check costs two minutes per gloss and saves you from
making confident claims in an essay about things AI made up.

:::tip[Without paid encyclopedia access]
If your university doesn't have the Oxford Classical Dictionary, free
alternatives exist: **Perseus Digital Library** (perseus.tufts.edu) for
Greek and Latin, with original texts and dictionaries; **Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (plato.stanford.edu) for philosophy; for
classical Latin **Castiglioni-Mariotti**, for Greek **Rocci** or LSJ
(Liddell-Scott-Jones, on Perseus).
:::

## Comparing editions

Reading two translations of the same text often says more than the
original: a translator's choices (what gets rendered, what gets
sacrificed, where they modernize) are a map of their critical angle.

AI speeds up the mapping: give it two translations of the same passage
and ask it to map the differences. A verifiable example is the *Iliad*
translated by Richmond Lattimore in 1951 and by Robert Fagles in 1990.
The prompt:

> *"I'm pasting two English translations of the first ten lines of
> the *Iliad*: the first is Richmond Lattimore (1951), the second is
> Robert Fagles (1990). What are the three most important
> differences, and what do they reveal about each translator's
> angle?"*

AI replies with a structured comparison: Lattimore's archaizing
fidelity and metrical strictness vs Fagles's modern flow and
performance-oriented rhythm, specific lexical choices (Homeric
epithets rendered differently), the line's pacing.

Always paste in the passages to compare, ten to thirty lines per side.
For very well-known works (*Iliad*, *Divine Comedy*, Shakespeare) AI has
pieces from memory but often gets the exact citations wrong: it invents a
hemistich or attributes a line to a different scene. AI doesn't replace
reading: it gives you the **map of differences**, which you then deepen
by reading the passages. The same pattern works for *Beowulf* (Seamus Heaney, 1999 vs J.R.R. Tolkien, 2014
posthumous), or for any classic with two translations distant in time.

For modern languages the pattern holds: two English translations of a
Proust novel (Moncrieff vs Davis), two versions of the same Borges story.
If only one English translation exists, compare it with a translation
into another language you know, or with the original even if you only
half-grasp the language: certain junctures show through anyway.

## Formal and informal register

For students of modern languages, the same sentence in two registers is
one of the most useful exercises. Classic example: an email to a
professor.

> *"I need to write to a professor in English to ask for an
> appointment. Give me two versions of the same email: a formal one
> (Anglo-American academic register, never met the person) and an
> informal one (I've met him before, friendly relationship).
> Underline the three differences that make the register."*

You see where it changes: greeting (*Dear Professor Smith* vs *Hi Prof*),
vocabulary (*I would like to* vs *I wanted to ask*), syntax (full
subordinates vs broken sentences), contractions (*I would* vs *I'd*). And
you learn to calibrate based on context.

It also works in reverse, to check naturalness: give AI a sentence of
yours and ask whether it sounds like a native speaker or like a textbook.

> *"Does this sentence sound natural in Parisian French as spoken
> by people in their thirties, or does it sound like a school
> textbook? If it's the second, tell me where it limps."*

Always declare the **regional variant** you're working in: Iberian or
Latin American Spanish, European or Brazilian Portuguese, British or
American English. AI doesn't guess it and mixes: it gives you a "neutral"
*coger* that in Argentina is vulgar, or a British *takeaway* in an email
to a university in Boston.

## Catullus, three versions and two glosses

A classics student works on the first line of *Carmen 5* by Catullus
(first century BC, the most famous <Def explanation="Latin poetic movement of the 1st century BC (Catullus is the most famous): short, lyrical, personal poetry, in opposition to traditional Roman civic epic.">Neoteric</Def> poet): *Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus*. She wants to
understand the translators' choices and the historical weight of the two
verbs.

<ChatDemo

Line: Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus.`}
  ai={`Three versions of the line *Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus*:

**Literal**: "Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love." Keeps the word order, the conjunction *atque* rendered as "and", the two hortatory subjunctives at the start and end of the period. Sounds spare, but it shows the structure.

**Idiomatic**: "Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love each other." Keeps the vocative in place, turns *amemus* from an absolute into a reciprocal. Smooth English, faithful to the sense.

**Free**: "To live, to love, Lesbia: this alone." Steps away from the hortatory subjunctive for a more direct exhortation, sacrifices the *vivamus / amemus* symmetry in favor of a clipped close. Betrays the form, saves the tone.

**Gloss on *vivamus***: the hortatory subjunctive "let us live" isn't a banality in Catullus. It fits into the Neoteric climate of claiming a private and sensual life **against** the civic ethic of the *mos maiorum* (the discipline inherited from the ancestors, made of public service and austerity). "To live" here has an almost programmatic value: choosing amorous *otium* as a horizon of meaning, not as a supplement to *negotium*. Close to Epicurean influences filtered through the Neoteric circle.

**Gloss on *amemus***: the reading is less unanimous. Literally "let us love" as a hortatory subjunctive, parallel with *vivamus*. Some philologists, however, read *amemus* as a collective imperative that draws Lesbia into a shared choice of life, rather than as a simple invitation to an act. The difference weighs on the translation: "let us love each other" (reciprocal act) vs "let us love" (way of being in the world). It's a nuance critical editions diverge on.`}
/>

</Collapsible>

Of the two glosses, one is solid and verifiable (*vivamus* in the
Neoteric and Epicurean frame) and the other carries an uncertainty AI
declares (the nuance on *amemus*). That declaration is the good sign: it
tells you where there's debate and where you have to check yourself
before backing a reading. The gloss on *mos maiorum*, before citing it in
an essay, you check on Treccani.

The worst case is when AI flags no uncertainty and is wrong anyway. Try
to find the errors in this answer.

An error like this slips by: the answer is fluent and the wrong sentences
sound like the others. Checking the gloss on Treccani or in a dictionary
of antiquities takes two minutes.

:::caution[How to reduce the risk]
Three countermeasures: ask which source (author, manual, dictionary) the
gloss comes from and check the source; ask the same gloss to a second AI
and compare; for every gloss that will end up in an essay, the
encyclopedic check isn't optional.
:::

<Collapsible title="Three linguistic traps to watch for">

**False friends.** Italian *attualmente* doesn't mean English *actually*
(it means "currently"). German *Gift* means "poison", not "gift". Spanish
*embarazada* is "pregnant", not "embarrassed". AI translates word by word
and often stumbles: if you're translating into a language you barely
know, check the key terms in a serious dictionary (Treccani, Larousse,
Duden, Oxford).

**Historical registers.** *Let* in Shakespeare covers a semantic field
we'd now call "hinder", not "permit". Same problem with Middle English
(Chaucer), medieval French, any text more than two or three centuries
old.

**Dialects and regional variants.** Iberian or Latin American Spanish,
European or Brazilian Portuguese, standard, Austrian or Swiss German.
Declare the variant: if you don't, AI mixes and you end up with a
"neutral" email that in a specific country sounds strange.

</Collapsible>

:::danger[AI in a translation exam is plagiarism]
In an exam, a translation assignment, or an interpreting test
(Latin, Greek, modern languages) AI isn't a support tool: it's a
**forbidden substitute**. Same thing as handing in a classmate's
translation. There are no gray zones on this point.

Where there is a gray zone instead: using AI to *study* (the three
versions, the glosses, the comparisons) is studying. Using AI to
prep the *specific passage* you think will come up on the exam is
preparing plagiarism: at the real test, on a new passage, you can't
translate anymore because you've trained the "what" and not the
"how". The wider ethical discussion is picked up by [*Ethics of
studying*](/en/for-students/ethics-of-studying/), the last lesson of
the module.
:::

## What NOT to do


**Don't study a text using a single translation.** A single translation
hides the choices the translator made and gives you the false certainty
that "the text says this".

**Don't trust historical glosses without an encyclopedic check.** They're
where AI invents most. Two minutes per gloss, and you don't sign off on
an invention.

**Don't use AI to translate into a language you don't know at all.** If
you have to send an email in Russian and you don't know a word of
Russian, AI gives you back the wrong register without you noticing. For
uses that matter (formal letter, application, publication), have a native
speaker review it.
