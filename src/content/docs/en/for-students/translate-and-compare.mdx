Translation is one of those areas where AI "looks perfect" and can
still be subtly wrong. A wrong translation of a line of Catullus, or of an
article from *Le Monde*, isn't visible at a glance: you only see it
when you compare it with other translations, with the original text,
or with a dictionary. The fluency of the answer tells you nothing
about its fidelity to the text.

This lesson shows how to use AI as a **help** for translation, not
as a substitute. It applies to people studying modern languages
(English, French, Spanish, German), to people doing classics (Latin,
Greek), to comparative literature, to <Def explanation="Rigorous study of ancient texts: reconstructing the original from copies, dating them, attributing them, interpreting their historical lexicon. It's the discipline that says 'this line is by Catullus, and here's the textual problem'.">philology</Def>. The mechanics are
the same: translation is a learning tool, not a finished object you
paste into the assignment.

## The three-versions pattern

Never ask "translate this sentence for me". Always ask for **three
versions with different angles**. For a sentence of French prose, a
line of verse, a long period:

- **Literal**: as close as possible to the original text, even at
  the cost of sounding unnatural in English. It keeps word order,
  lexical choices, syntactic constructions.
- **Idiomatic**: smooth English, small syntactic liberties to avoid
  betraying the meaning. It's the translation you'd find in a good
  current edition.
- **Free**: the idea comes through, but the phrasing changes. More
  adaptation than translation, useful for seeing where you can
  "break" the text without losing it.

The difference between the three shows you what you "lose" in
translation, and that's the real learning. People who translate
seriously don't pick the best one: they recompose a version of their
own by reading the three. People studying a language use the three
versions to surface grammatical structures that a single translation
hides (a French participle that becomes a subordinate clause in
idiomatic English, a Latin ablative that unfolds into a whole
sentence).

The base prompt, to adapt:

> *"Translate this sentence in three versions: a literal one, an
> idiomatic one, a free one. For each one, explain in one line the
> main choice you made."*

The line of explanation matters as much as the translations: it's
where AI shows you its move. If the three versions come back very
similar to one another, the text is "transparent" between the two
languages (low syntactic density, close vocabulary): that's not a problem, it's
information. If instead you suspect laziness, ask explicitly
*"push the differences, even take them to extremes"*.

## Glosses and historical notes

For texts that need historical understanding (Latin, Greek, Old
Italian, Old French, Old English) a modern translation isn't
enough. A word may have no equivalent, or have one but with a
different historical meaning. *Let* in Shakespeare often means
"hinder", not "permit": *"I'll make a ghost of him that lets me"* in
*Hamlet* trips up a modern reader who only knows the current sense.
*Patroni* in a Roman context isn't the "boss" of modern vocabulary.

The move is to ask AI to **pair the translation with glosses**:
words or concepts a modern reader doesn't recognize, explained in
two lines. Like:

> *"Translate this line of Catullus for me. For the terms *patroni*
> and *mos maiorum*, add a two-line gloss on the historical value in
> the Roman context of the first century BC."*

Important caution. Historical glosses are one of the points where
AI invents most readily: wrong dates, attributions to authors who
never existed, garbled etymologies. It's the
structural limit of a model that has read a lot but doesn't query a
database while it responds. Verify at least one gloss out of two
with a serious encyclopedic source, and prioritize the riskiest:
proper names, dates, work titles, etymologies. Generic definitions
of well-known concepts you can verify last. **Treccani** (the major
Italian encyclopedia, free online) for Italian and historical
concepts, **Oxford Classical Dictionary** for the classics (your
library almost certainly has access).

The check costs two minutes per gloss, and it saves you from making
confident claims in an essay about things AI made up.

:::tip[Without paid encyclopedia access]
If your university doesn't have access to the Oxford Classical
Dictionary or other commercial encyclopedias, free alternatives are
there. **Perseus Digital Library** (perseus.tufts.edu) for Greek and
Latin: original texts, dictionaries, clickable lemmas. **Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (plato.stanford.edu) for ancient and
modern philosophy, articles written by specialists. English-language
Wikipedia is often denser than the Italian one for the classics. For
classical Latin, **Castiglioni-Mariotti** (Italian Latin
dictionary); for Greek, **Rocci** (Italian Greek dictionary) or LSJ
(Liddell-Scott-Jones, on Perseus).
:::

## Comparing editions

For a student of comparative literature or classics, reading two
translations of the same text often says more than the original
text. A translator's choices (what gets rendered, what gets
sacrificed, where they modernize, where they let the ancient grate)
are a map of their critical angle.

AI speeds up the mapping. Pattern: give AI two translations of the
same passage and ask it to map the differences. Real and verifiable
example: the *Iliad* translated by Richmond Lattimore in 1951 and
by Robert Fagles in 1990. The prompt:

> *"I'm pasting two English translations of the first ten lines of
> the *Iliad*: the first is Richmond Lattimore (1951), the second is
> Robert Fagles (1990). What are the three most important
> differences, and what do they reveal about each translator's
> angle?"*

AI replies with a structured comparison: Lattimore's archaizing
fidelity and metrical strictness vs Fagles's modern flow and
performance-oriented rhythm, specific lexical choices (Homeric
epithets rendered differently), the line's pacing.

Always paste in
the passages you want to compare, ten to thirty lines per side. For
very well-known works (*Iliad*, *Divine Comedy*, Shakespeare) AI
has pieces from memory but often gets the exact citations wrong:
it invents a hemistich, attributes a line to a different scene. For
modern or niche works, pasting is mandatory.

AI doesn't replace reading: it gives you the **map of differences**,
which you then deepen by reading the passages. The same pattern
works for *Beowulf* in English (Seamus Heaney, 1999 vs J.R.R.
Tolkien, 2014 posthumous), or for any classic with two translations
distant in time.

For people doing modern languages the pattern holds: two English
translations of a Proust novel (Moncrieff vs Davis), two editions
of a Sontag essay, two versions of the same Borges story. If only
one English translation of a text exists, compare it with a
translation into another language you know (French, German,
Italian), or with the original text even if you only half-grasp the
language: certain junctures show through anyway.

## Formal and informal register

For students of modern languages, the same sentence in two registers
is one of the most useful exercises. Classic example: an email to a
professor.

Prompt:

> *"I need to write to a professor in English to ask for an
> appointment. Give me two versions of the same email: a formal one
> (Anglo-American academic register, never met the person) and an
> informal one (I've met him before, friendly relationship).
> Underline the three differences that make the register."*

You see where it changes: greeting (*Dear Professor Smith* vs *Hi
Prof*), vocabulary (*I would like to* vs *I wanted to ask*, *kindly*
vs *could you*), syntax (full subordinates vs broken sentences),
contractions (*I would* vs *I'd*). And you learn to calibrate based
on context.

It also works in reverse, to check naturalness: give AI a sentence
of yours and ask whether it sounds like a native speaker or like a
textbook.

> *"Does this sentence sound natural in Parisian French as spoken
> by people in their thirties, or does it sound like a school
> textbook? If it's the second, tell me where it limps."*

Important: always declare the **regional variant** you're working
in. Iberian or Latin American Spanish (and which country)? European
or Brazilian Portuguese? British, American, or Australian English?
AI doesn't guess it, and it mixes: it gives you a "neutral" *coger*
that in Argentina is vulgar, or a British *takeaway* in an email
sent to a university in Boston.

## Catullus, three versions and two glosses

Classics student, working on the first line of *Carmen 5* by Catullus
(first century BC, the most famous <Def explanation="Latin poetic movement of the 1st century BC (Catullus is the most famous): short, lyrical, personal poetry, in opposition to traditional Roman civic epic.">Neoteric</Def> poet): *Vivamus, mea
Lesbia, atque amemus*. She wants to understand the translators'
choices and the historical weight of the two verbs.

<ChatDemo
  user={`Translate this line of Catullus into English in three versions: literal, idiomatic, free. Then add two short historical glosses on the terms "vivamus" and "amemus" in Catullus's poetic context (Neoteric poetry, first century BC).

Line: Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus.`}
  ai={`Three versions of the line *Vivamus, mea Lesbia, atque amemus*:

**Literal**: "Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love." Keeps the word order, the conjunction *atque* rendered as "and", the two hortatory subjunctives at the start and end of the period. Sounds spare, but it shows the structure.

**Idiomatic**: "Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love each other." Keeps the vocative in place, turns *amemus* from an absolute into a reciprocal. Smooth English, faithful to the sense.

**Free**: "To live, to love, Lesbia: this alone." Steps away from the hortatory subjunctive for a more direct exhortation, sacrifices the *vivamus / amemus* symmetry in favor of a clipped close. Betrays the form, saves the tone.

**Gloss on *vivamus***: the hortatory subjunctive "let us live" isn't a banality in Catullus. It fits into the Neoteric climate of claiming a private and sensual life **against** the civic ethic of the *mos maiorum* (the discipline inherited from the ancestors, made of public service and austerity). "To live" here has an almost programmatic value: choosing amorous *otium* as a horizon of meaning, not as a supplement to *negotium*. Close to Epicurean influences filtered through the Neoteric circle.

**Gloss on *amemus***: the reading is less unanimous. Literally "let us love" as a hortatory subjunctive, parallel with *vivamus*. Some philologists, however, read *amemus* as a collective imperative that draws Lesbia into a shared choice of life, rather than as a simple invitation to an act. The difference weighs on the translation: "let us love each other" (reciprocal act) vs "let us love" (way of being in the world). It's a nuance critical editions diverge on.`}
/>

Worth noting: of the two glosses, one is solid and verifiable (the
one on *vivamus* in the Neoteric and Epicurean frame) and the other
carries an uncertainty AI declares explicitly (the nuance on
*amemus*). That declaration of uncertainty is the
good sign: it tells you where there's debate, and where you have to
check yourself before backing a reading. The gloss on *mos maiorum*,
before citing it in an essay, you check on Treccani.

:::caution[When the AI doesn't flag uncertainty but is wrong]
The good sign is when AI flags where there's debate (like the gloss
on *amemus*). The problem is when it *doesn't* and gets it wrong
anyway: you aren't an expert yet, and you don't have the signal to
stop. Three countermeasures: always ask which source (author,
manual, dictionary) the gloss comes from, then check the source; ask
the same gloss to a second AI (Claude vs ChatGPT vs Gemini) and
compare; for every gloss that will end up in an essay, the
encyclopedic check isn't optional.
:::

:::caution[Linguistic traps]
Three types of trap AI often falls into, and where you have to keep
watch.

**False friends.** Italian *attualmente* doesn't mean English
*actually* (it means "currently"). German *Gift* means "poison", not
"gift" as in English. Spanish *embarazada* is "pregnant", not
"embarrassed". AI translates word by word and often stumbles. If
you're translating into a language you barely know, check the key
terms in a serious dictionary (Treccani, Larousse, Duden, Oxford).

**Historical registers.** A sixteenth-century English expression
doesn't mean what the same expression means today. *Let* in
Shakespeare covers a semantic field we'd now call "hinder", not
"permit". Same problem with Middle English (Chaucer), medieval
French, any text more than two or three centuries old.

**Dialects and regional variants.** Iberian vs Latin American
Spanish. European vs Brazilian Portuguese. British vs American vs
Australian English. Standard vs Austrian vs Swiss German. Always
declare the variant to AI: if you don't, it mixes, and you end up
with a "neutral" email that in a specific country sounds strange.
:::

:::danger[AI in a translation exam is plagiarism]
In an exam, a translation assignment, or an interpreting test
(Latin, Greek, modern languages) AI isn't a support tool: it's a
**forbidden substitute**. Same thing as handing in a classmate's
translation. There are no gray zones on this point.

Where there is a gray zone instead: using AI to *study* (the three
versions, the glosses, the comparisons) is studying. Using AI to
prep the *specific passage* you think will come up on the exam is
preparing plagiarism: at the real test, on a new passage, you can't
translate anymore because you've trained the "what" and not the
"how". The wider ethical discussion is picked up by [*Ethics of
studying*](/en/for-students/ethics-of-studying/), the last lesson of
the module.
:::

## What NOT to do

**Don't hand in an AI translation in a translation exam.** It's
plagiarism, and falls under the same sanctions as any other form of
plagiarism.

**Don't study a text using a single translation.** This whole lesson
is about comparison: three angles for the same sentence, two
editions side by side, two registers. A single translation hides the
choices the translator made, and gives you the false certainty that
"the text says this".

**Don't trust historical glosses without an encyclopedic check.**
They're the point where AI invents most: dates, attributions,
etymologies. Treccani, Oxford Classical Dictionary, or a reference
history of your field: two minutes per gloss, and you don't sign off
on an invention.

**Don't use AI to translate into a language you don't know at all.**
If you have to send an email in Russian and you don't know a word of
Russian, AI gives you back an email in the wrong register without
you noticing. For uses that matter (formal letter, application,
publication), always have a native speaker review it.
