This module teaches you to study better. Getting your homework done for
you doesn't teach you anything and falls apart at the first oral exam.
AI is a study tool when it explains things, quizzes you, helps you
review, and a problem when it produces, in your place, the text you're
supposed to hand in. The final lesson, [*Ethics of
studying*](/en/for-students/ethics-of-studying/), comes back to where
the line falls.

## From curiosity to formal study

In [*Learn something new*](/en/everyday-use/learn-something-new/)
you saw how to use the AI to understand a topic that interests
you: blockchain, inflation, bacterial genetics. There the stakes
were "did I get it or not?", and you alone were enough to judge.

Formal study changes three things:

- There's a **reference material**, picked by the course: often a mix of
  chapters, handouts, slides and articles. What matters is what *that*
  material says, not how the topic is told in general on the internet.
- There's a **professor who decides what counts as "right"**, through the
  material and through what they add out loud. Definitions can shift
  between authors and between professors: in your course the professor's
  version wins, even when it's in tension with the textbook.
- There's **a test at the end**: oral, written, paper. Someone will ask
  you precise questions and expects answers with the course's vocabulary.

The AI knows none of the three. Left to its defaults, it fishes from
Wikipedia, popular articles, English-language textbooks, and gives you
back an almost-right mix that doesn't match your syllabus. You have to
anchor it to your material and calibrate it to your professor: in
[*Learn something new*](/en/everyday-use/learn-something-new/) you used
it freely, here you put it on a leash.

## Three techniques to anchor the AI to your syllabus

The three techniques anchor the AI to the **content** of your syllabus,
to the **type of questions** you expect and to the **professor's style**.
They combine: paste the paragraph, ask exam-style questions on it,
attach style notes.

### 1. Paste the textbook paragraph

The simplest move and the most underrated. When you need to understand a
concept from the course, don't ask "explain X to me": copy the paragraph
(or the slide bullet) that introduces it, paste it into the chat, and
*then* ask for the explanation while staying inside that frame.

The prompt looks different. From:

> *"Explain the dissolution powers of the President of the
> Republic to me."*

To:

> *"[textbook paragraph pasted]. Explain this paragraph to me in
> simpler words, but stay inside the frame the text gives. If you
> add something that isn't here, flag it for me."*

The difference shows right away. In the first version the AI gives you an
averaged summary: definitions floating around the web, some common sense.
In the second it's forced to reformulate what's in front of you. If the
textbook insists on a distinction (for example between "formally
presidential acts" and "substantively presidential acts"), the
explanation keeps it. If you ask in general, that nuance disappears and
at the exam you're missing it.

The same goes for slides: if the professor put a definition on a slide,
that's the reference. If the slide is just three-word bullets, add a line
at the end of the prompt with what the professor said out loud on that
point.

:::tip[How much to paste at a time]
One paragraph (half a page) at a time works better than three full
pages. If the topic is big, split it: anchor the AI to the first block,
understand it, then move to the second.
:::

### 2. Ask exam-style questions on that chapter

Once you've understood the concept, flip the roles (the "return
questioning" technique from [*Learn something
new*](/en/everyday-use/learn-something-new/)) and force the level of the
exam.

> *"Based on the chapter I pasted above, give me three oral-style
> questions and three written-style questions, at the level of
> precision expected by a constitutional law exam in the second
> year of law school. One at a time, wait for my answer before
> moving to the next. After each answer tell me if it's correct
> and what's missing to get a top grade."*

The AI changes tone. Without the level spec, the questions come out
generic ("what is the President of the Republic for?"); with it, they
come out precise ("distinguish between presidential acts that require
ministerial countersignature and those that don't, with one example for
each"). Oral and written are different tests: the oral rewards synthesis
and reasoning out loud, the written precision and completeness.
Practicing both gives you different angles on the same material.

### 3. Calibrate the AI to your professor's style

This move matters when the exam gets close. If you have lecture notes,
the professor's slides, or even just the outline of a past oral exam
circulating among students, paste it in as a style reference.

> *"These are notes from three lectures by my professor. They
> tend to insist on X and Y, and often ask questions like Z.
> When you quiz me on this chapter, simulate their way of asking,
> not a generic quiz."*

The AI doesn't know your professor by name, but it picks up on the
*pattern*: if they ask for practical applications it gets that from the
notes, if they ask for word-for-word definitions that comes out too. The
simulation isn't perfect, but it gets you closer to the classroom than a
random quiz from the internet.

An example: you're preparing a modern history exam and from the notes of
the last three lectures your professor has insisted on the role of the
press between the two world wars, often citing Mosse and Gentile. The
prompt becomes:

> *"I'm studying for a modern history exam. In the notes I'm
> pasting in, my professor insists on two things: the role of the
> press between the two world wars and the authors Mosse and Gentile.
> When you quiz me on the chapter I'm passing you right after,
> simulate their way of asking: questions that connect the general
> theme to these two authors, one at a time. Notes: \[paste 1-2 pages
> of notes]."*

On the lead of the two authors, the AI asks "how would Mosse explain this
passage from the textbook?" instead of the definition of a term.

:::note[For STEM students]
The three techniques apply here too, with a tweak: instead of the prose
paragraph you paste the **theorem** with its proof, the **exercise** with
its solution, or the **passage** that introduces the formula. The prompt:
"explain this proof to me step by step, staying inside the notation the
text uses. If you add a step that isn't here, flag it for me." For the
professor's style, the "style notes" become past exercises or previous
exam papers.
:::

## The difference in practice

A constitutional law student, with the Bin/Pitruzzella textbook, is
studying the powers of the President of the Republic. First try, asking
in general.

The answer is honest, but the comparison with the United States is side

It also misses a distinction that Bin/Pitruzzella develop with care, on
the substantively or formally presidential nature of the dissolution act:
exactly the nuance constitutional law professors insist on.

**Who actually decides.** The textbook tells you that behind the form there's a different substance. According to the majority doctrine, the political decision to dissolve belongs to the Government, which proposes it with the countersignature of the President of the Council of Ministers. The President of the Republic carries out a check on legality (is it lawful?) and on timing (is this the right moment?), but it's not them who politically chooses to go to elections. Three differences compared to the general answer. The vocabulary is the
textbook's ("formally presidential but substantively complex act"),
which is what the professor expects to hear. There's no comparison with
the United States, which isn't in the paragraph. And the AI flags the
point the paragraph leaves out, so you know where to look for the rest.

A quick test if you suspect it's drifting off: ask "which part of the
paragraph I pasted is this sentence based on?".

## "I get it" is not "I know what the professor expects"

There's a specific risk: you hold a nice conversation, the AI
compliments you, you leave convinced you know the topic. Then the
professor asks their question and you freeze. In a conversation you can
wander and let the chat context feed you the words you're missing. At the
oral exam you're alone, with a professor waiting for an answer that uses
the textbook's words.

A quick check at the end of a session: close the chat, open the book to
the chapter, close that too. Explain the topic out loud, alone, as if you
were at the oral. If you stumble, mark the exact point: usually it's the
vocabulary (you're missing the textbook's technical word) or the
connection (you know the individual facts but not how they fit together).
For the vocabulary you go back to the textbook and write the definitions
out by hand. For the connection you ask the AI for three connection
questions on that chapter. It's a variant of the <Def explanation="Study method: you explain a topic out loud as if you were teaching it to a child. Where you stumble, that's where you don't really know it.">Feynman
technique</Def>, applied to the course syllabus.

:::caution[Your professor's material and privacy]
Uploading lecture notes, slides or recordings to a chat means sending
that material to the AI's servers. If it's covered by academic copyright
(often the case for unpublished slides and handouts), it's a choice at
your own risk. For sensitive material disable the history in your tool's
privacy settings, or retype the passages you need by hand. The bigger
picture is in [*What you share when you use the
AI*](/en/fundamentals/privacy-basics/).
:::

## What NOT to do

**Don't stop at "explain it to me easy".** The AI simplifies happily, but
the exam asks for the level of the course. The easy explanation is the
first step: after it, go back to the textbook's technical vocabulary.

**Don't use the AI's voice instead of your own at the oral.** Answers
built with the AI carry the [polished AI tone](/en/everyday-use/write-better/#the-average-voice), and at the oral they sound
fake. The professor tells the difference between an answer you've chewed
through yourself, even with some stumbles, and one recited with a chatbot
cadence. Study with the AI, answer with your own head.
