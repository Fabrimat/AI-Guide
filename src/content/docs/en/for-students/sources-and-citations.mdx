The academic bibliography is where AI does the most damage. It has
learned to "sound like a bibliography" very well without producing a
real one: the citations it offers have an author with a plausible
surname, a journal that exists, a recent year, credible page numbers.
The specific combination just often doesn't exist.

Telling a fake citation from a real one is a skill, and it can be
learned in one sitting. If you don't learn it, your supervisor picks a
random citation from your bibliography, looks up the DOI, and figures
out that you've handed in citations that never existed.

## Anatomy of a fabricated citation

AI has been trained on millions of real citations and has learned the
form very well, but "producing a plausible citation" and "producing a
citation that exists" are the same task as far as it's concerned: which
combination actually exists sits in a database it doesn't query while it
responds. So it invents with the same structure as real citations: a
well-known academic in the field, an existing journal or close to it, a
recent year. What's missing is the match: the author, title, journal and
year combination doesn't exist, or the DOI doesn't resolve, or the
article exists but says something different from what the citation
attributes to it.

Four typical fabrication patterns worth keeping in mind.

**Real author + real paper + wrong year.** The author and the paper
exist, but the citation dates it to 2018 when it's from 2014.

**Real author + non-existent paper.** A well-respected scholar, cited as
the author of an article they never wrote, with a plausible title. You
believe it because you know the author.

**Real journal + non-existent issue.** The *Journal of Health Economics*
exists, but volume 67 issue 3 from 2019 doesn't contain the article
cited.

**Perfect and fabricated.** Invented author, paper, journal and DOI,
with an impeccable form. It's the most frequent case when you ask an AI
without web search for "three recent papers on X".

## The DOI as a verification gate

Almost every academic article published since 2000 has a DOI, *Digital
Object Identifier*: a string that starts with `10.`, followed by a
publisher identifier, a slash and a code. For example
`10.1016/j.jhealeco.2019.102225` is the DOI of an article by Cawley et
al. from 2019 in the *Journal of Health Economics*, which you'll see in
the example further down.

The check is fast: paste the DOI onto `doi.org/<DOI>` (for example
`https://doi.org/10.1086/675329`) or look it up on Google Scholar. Three
possible outcomes.

**The DOI doesn't resolve.** The page says the document doesn't exist.
The citation is almost certainly made up: throw it out.

**The DOI resolves to a different paper than expected.** If title,
authors and year don't match, AI has attributed the wrong content to a
real identifier: throw it out.

**The DOI resolves to the right paper.** The citation is almost
certainly real. What remains is checking that the paper actually says
what AI attributes to it: for that you need at least the abstract.

The DOI is the fastest filter you have: ten seconds per citation. For
pre-2000 articles and books, which often have no DOI, search author and
title on WorldCat or your library's catalog: if nothing comes up, check
the citation more carefully.

:::tip[AI with web access]
Some AIs have real-time web access: ChatGPT with web search, Claude with
web search, Gemini, Perplexity. With these you can add to your prompt
*"verify every DOI on doi.org before giving it to me, and discard the
ones that don't resolve"*: the rate of fabricated citations drops a lot.
With an AI without web access the same instruction is useless: it will
simulate the check and invent that too.
:::

## Four citation styles

Four main styles circulate in academic work, depending on the field. If
your supervisor doesn't specify, ask: one email line settles it.
Otherwise follow your department's convention, or leaf through two or
three recent theses your supervisor has followed.

<Collapsible title="The four styles in brief">

**APA**: social sciences and humanities (psychology, sociology,
economics, education). In-text `(Author, year)`, bibliography ordered by
surname.

**MLA**: Anglo humanities (literature, languages, cultural studies).
In-text `(Author, page)`, bibliography called "Works Cited".

**Chicago**: history and some Italian humanities. Footnotes with the
full citation the first time, shortened afterwards.

**ISO 690**: technical sciences and engineering theses. Numbered
references `[1], [2], ...` in the text, bibliography in the same order.

</Collapsible>

For any style, AI is a good **formatter**: you give it the raw data
(author, title, year, pages, journal) and ask *"format these five
entries in Chicago style"*. It isn't reliable at remembering the exact
rules: it can get a detail wrong (a comma instead of a period, italics
on the wrong title). To check, use an official guide (Purdue OWL is the
strongest free reference) or, better, **Zotero**, free and open source.
You install the app and the browser extension, and from a paper's page
you import author, title, year, journal and DOI with one click. Then you
format in any style without asking AI for anything.

## Real academic source or dressed-up blog

When you ask AI to "find me sources on X", fabrication isn't the only
risk. There's also confusion of register: it offers you as academic
things that only look it. Three typical cases:

Three typical cases:

- articles on `medium.com` with a scholarly tone and internal
  citations;
- blogs by doctoral students or research centers, well written but
  not peer-reviewed;
- <Def explanation="Draft of an academic article posted online before peer review. It can be revised or withdrawn, but it saves the months of waiting for journal publication.">preprints</Def> on SSRN or ResearchGate not yet published in a journal,
  which can still be modified or withdrawn.

None of this is junk: a good blog can be informed, a preprint can be a
serious piece of work waiting for publication. But using them in a
thesis as if they were peer-reviewed is a mistake that your supervisor
will flag immediately.

Three practical filters to tell them apart.

1. **Resolvable DOI.** The gate seen above. If there's no DOI, or
   there is one but it doesn't resolve, the source doesn't pass the
   academic filter.

2. **Indexed journal.** Search the journal title on `scopus.com` or
   Web of Science (free through your library's proxy: the system
   that lets you, from the campus Wi-Fi or from home logging in with
   student credentials, access databases your university subscribes
   to). If the journal is <Def explanation="Included in an official academic database (Scopus, Web of Science) that certifies a journal as peer-reviewed and recognized in its field. Not every 'serious' journal is indexed, but indexed journals are almost always serious.">indexed</Def> in at least one of the two, it's a
   peer-reviewed journal recognized in its field.

3. **Known publisher.** Springer, Elsevier, Cambridge University
   Press, Oxford University Press, JSTOR, Taylor & Francis, the
   university presses of major universities. If the publisher is an
   unknown and the DOI doesn't resolve, be suspicious.

Two filters out of three passed: the source is probably solid. Zero
filters out of three: you're citing a blog, not a paper.

:::note[Institutional sources: a separate case]
Reports from Banca d'Italia (the Bank of Italy), OECD, ISTAT, Eurostat,
World Bank don't pass the three filters: they're not peer-reviewed, they
rarely have a DOI, the publisher is an institution. And yet they're
authoritative sources, especially in economics and social sciences. They
are cited with author = the institution (e.g., *Banca d'Italia*), year,
report title and stable URL, often in a separate "Institutional sources"
section, and judged by the authority of the issuing body.
:::

<Collapsible title="NotebookLM and similar: working on a closed bibliography">

**NotebookLM** is a Google tool built to work on a bibliography you
already have. You upload the PDFs of the papers you've selected and AI
answers **only** based on those documents, citing which paper each
sentence came from. By construction it won't offer content that isn't in
the uploaded documents, so no invented citations.

It's the safest way to work when the bibliography is settled: a thesis on
an author whose twenty key articles you already have, a chapter based on
a known set of papers. The limitation is structural: you have to already
have the papers. NotebookLM replaces the synthesis phase, not the source
discovery phase.

Similar tools are Claude and ChatGPT **Projects**, and Custom GPTs with a
knowledge base: more flexible for brainstorming and writing, less
specialized in citing the source. The constraint from [*Research for
work*](/en/at-work/research-for-work/) applies: before you upload PDFs of
paywalled papers or copyrighted material, check that the platform is
appropriate and that you aren't violating the journal's license.

</Collapsible>

## Three papers on taxation and food consumption

Economics student, working on a term paper. Asks AI for three
recent papers on a specific topic. AI replies with three
well-formatted citations, APA style, complete with DOIs.

<ChatDemo
  user={`Find me three recent papers (last five years) on the effect of taxation on food consumption, particularly on sugar-sweetened beverage taxes. APA style, include the DOI for each.`}
  ai={`Here are three papers on the topic:

1. **Marinelli, A. & Rossi, P. (2022).** Sugar tax and consumption patterns in Southern Europe: evidence from a natural experiment. *Food Policy*, 109, 102234. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodpol.2022.102234

2. **Schmidt, K. & Weber, J. (2021).** The pass-through of sugar-sweetened beverage taxes to consumer prices: a cross-country analysis. *Journal of Public Economics*, 198, 104412. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2021.104412

3. **Cawley, J., Frisvold, D., Hill, A. & Jones, D. (2019).** The impact of the Philadelphia beverage tax on purchases and consumption by adults and children. *Journal of Health Economics*, 67, 102225. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2019.102225`}
/>

The student opens `doi.org` and tries the three DOIs. The first doesn't
resolve. The second opens a paper on a completely different topic,
electric cars and public incentives in Norway. The third opens exactly
the Cawley et al. 2019 article on the *Philadelphia beverage tax*. Two
of the three citations are made up: one with a fake DOI, one with a DOI
that leads somewhere else.

The next move, to paste into the same chat: *"I've verified. The first
DOI doesn't resolve, the second opens a paper on a completely different
topic. Let's only work on the third, Cawley et al. 2019. Add me three
recent papers that cite or extend that one, and give me verifiable DOIs
or tell me if you don't have any with certainty."* AI now has a real
anchor point and an explicit safety exit: it can say "I don't have
reliable recent sources". It's the same pattern as [*Research for
work*](/en/at-work/research-for-work/): give AI an exit to admit the
limit, and it invents less.

## What NOT to do

**Don't hand in your bibliography without verifying every DOI.** Your
supervisor picks one at random and unmasks you. Opening ten links on
`doi.org` costs two minutes.

**Don't use "find me recent papers" as a primary source.** For real
research you start from Google Scholar or your university's database
(Scopus, JSTOR, ProQuest, depending on the field). AI is fine as a
second step, when you already have a core of papers and want to format,
synthesize, or look for connections.

**Don't trust AI when it says "this paper argues X".** Even if the DOI
resolves to the right paper, it can attribute a claim the paper doesn't
support. Read at least the abstract and, for papers that go into the
bibliography, the paper.
