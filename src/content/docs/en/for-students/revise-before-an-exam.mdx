You've studied the syllabus with the techniques from [*Study a new
topic*](/en/for-students/study-a-new-topic/) and composed the material
with those from [*Notes from lectures and long
texts*](/en/for-students/notes-from-lectures/). Two weeks left until
the exam. You know the material, but the professor will ask you about
it on your feet, under pressure, at their pace.

Studying and revising are two different jobs. Studying is understanding
the first time. Revising is repeating what you already know until the
answer comes out fluently under exam conditions. The textbook alone
doesn't generate those conditions: you open it, you read, you nod
along. AI does: you can put it in the professor's shoes and have it
question you as many times as you want, even at three in the morning.

## The three techniques

### 1. Simulated oral exam

The central move of revision. The core prompt:

> *"Pretend to be my [subject] professor at the oral exam. Here's the
> course syllabus: [paste the textbook index or the list of topics].
> Ask me one question at a time, don't move to the next one until I've
> answered. After my answer, give me an honest verdict (accurate /
> imprecise / missing), and if it's imprecise tell me what you would
> have wanted to hear. Don't move to the final grade until I ask you
> to."*

Three specifications make the difference. **One question at a time**:
without this, AI fires four questions in a block and you lose the
pressure of the face-to-face. **Honest verdict on every answer**: by
default AI wants to please and accepts vagueness with a "good answer,
let's move on". **No final grade until you ask for it**: without this,
after three questions it closes with a B+ and you walk away happy.

The syllabus pasted into the prompt prevents AI from pulling topics
from Wikipedia that your course doesn't cover. If there's no single
textbook, reconstruct the list from lecture and slide titles: fifteen
to twenty concise entries are enough to define the perimeter.

A good session lasts forty minutes, five to eight questions: after
that your attention drops and AI starts recycling similar questions.
Two sessions on different days work better than one two-hour marathon.
You're ready on a chapter when you answer three questions in a row
without AI having to correct you on anything substantial. Below that
threshold, go back to the textbook.

### 2. Written test with correction

For written exams the pattern is the same, but you produce a text and
AI corrects you at the point where you went wrong.

> *"Give me a typical [subject] written exam prompt on chapter X. I'll
> answer it, then you give me an honest verdict: where I wrote
> correct things, where I was imprecise, what I should have added. If
> I got a specific step wrong, show me the correct step, not just the
> final result."*

It works the same way for technical problems in math, physics,
engineering. Ask for the correction of the **step**: knowing that "the
result is 12 and not 14" doesn't help you avoid the same mistake,
knowing that you misapplied the chain rule at the third step does.

For multiple-choice tests ask for ten or fifteen questions in the style
of your exam, with four alternatives. Answer before looking at the
solution, and for each wrong one ask *why* the others were wrong.
Without the explanation of *why not* the distractors, multiple choice
becomes pattern memorization and you don't grasp the structure of the
question.

### 3. Mind maps and flashcards

Two tools complementary to questioned revision.

The **mind map** is a hierarchical diagram: main concept at the top,
sub-concepts branching out. It's useful before the oral exam to "see"
the structure of the chapter at a glance. Ask: *"give me a textual
mind map of chapter X, main concept at the top, four or five
branches, two or three sub-concepts per branch."* The output is an
indented outline. If you prefer the graphical version, paste it into
XMind, MindMeister, or Excalidraw.

**Flashcards** are the opposite of the map: no structure, many short
questions on single concepts, to be repeated at growing intervals of
time (the principle of *spaced repetition*: you review a card when
you're about to forget it, so each repetition costs less than the
previous one).

<Collapsible title="Flashcards: Anki, Quizlet and the CSV format">

**Anki** and **Quizlet** are the two standard tools. Anki is free and
open source; Quizlet is friendlier, with a more limited free version.
Both import text files: `Question,Answer` (CSV) or `Question<TAB>Answer`
(TSV), one card per line.

Ask AI: *"give me twenty flashcards on chapter X in CSV format, one per
line, comma as separator, no headers. If an answer contains commas, wrap
it in double quotes. Focus on definitions, dates, names, and key
formulas."* You copy the output, save it as `.csv`, import it.

Real limit: AI tends to ask questions that are too easy ("Who was
Kant?") or too open-ended ("Explain the categorical imperative"). Check
the first ten cards before importing everything; if they don't work,
send AI back with an example of a good question against a bad one.

</Collapsible>

## Strategy for the professor

Two typical profiles: the techniques stay the same, the calibration
changes.

**The predictable professor.** Every year asks about the same topics,
with a recognizable type of question. If you have questions from past
exams, even just the ones circulating in the course's WhatsApp groups,
paste them in: *"this is the style of questions the professor asks.
When you question me, simulate it."* It's the calibration of the third
technique of [*Study a new topic*](/en/for-students/study-a-new-topic/),
carried over into revision.

**The unpredictable professor.** Asks reasoning questions: knowing
chapter 4 by heart won't save you, they'll ask you to link it to
chapter 7. Here AI generates **transversal** questions: *"give me five
questions that connect chapter 4 with chapter 7, the kind a professor
would ask to see if you've integrated the syllabus."* They force you to
build bridges you wouldn't build reading in order.

At the first exam with a professor, with no hints about their style, go
with the unpredictable profile: connecting questions require knowing the
syllabus more deeply, and they also cover the case where the professor
turns out to be predictable.

## A philosophy oral exam

A theoretical philosophy student has the exam in a week. The professor
is predictable and insists on Heidegger, in particular on the concept of
*aletheia*. The student sets up the prompt of Technique 1 with the course
syllabus, AI asks the first question and the student answers.

<ChatDemo
  user={`For Heidegger, aletheia is the Greek concept of truth. The word literally means "unconcealment", and for Heidegger truth is not the correspondence between a proposition and a thing, but the way being manifests itself. Truth understood as idea, in the Platonic sense, is a consequence of this originary manifestation.`}
  ai={`**Accurate** on two points: the etymology of *aletheia* as "unconcealment" (more precisely *a-letheia*, privative of *lethe*, concealment) and the critique of truth as correspondence.

**Imprecise** on the last part. You said that "truth understood as idea, in the Platonic sense, is a consequence of this originary manifestation". Here you trip on one of the points the professor always comes back to. Heidegger doesn't say that Platonic truth as *idea* derives from *aletheia*: he says it *replaces* it, and that this replacement is the first fall of Western metaphysics. For Heidegger, Plato is the moment when truth stops being *disclosure* (event, happening of being) and becomes *adequation* of a gaze to an ideal form. The originary *aletheia* is covered over by the idea, not prolonged in it. You should have said: "truth as *disclosure* is what Plato, according to Heidegger, loses by introducing the idea as the measure of the true". Not a small difference: in your formulation Plato is an heir to Heidegger, in the correct formulation he is the beginning of his polemical target.

When you're ready I'll move on to the second question.`}
/>

</Collapsible>

The verdict isn't a generic "good answer": it's split into accurate and
imprecise, with the imprecise zone pointed out down to the millimeter.
The correction tells you *what you should have said*, not just "no". And
AI stops: it doesn't move to the second question until the student asks.

:::caution[AI that confirms you is the risk]
AI tends to agree with you. If you answer something vague but "in the
ballpark", the default response is "yes, you got the point", followed by
an addition that fills in the gaps you left. You walk out convinced you
knew, but you knew vaguely: it supplied the pieces you were missing.

Three countermeasures. The first: in the prompt, ask for a strict
verdict (*"if I answer vaguely, tell me it's vague, don't fill in the
gaps yourself"*). The second, the most important: re-check against the
textbook the key points where AI corrected you. A correction that sounds
right can be a hallucination, especially on authors, quotes and dates:
the same rule applies as in [*When to trust it and when
not*](/en/fundamentals/when-to-trust-it/). The third, optional: for the
points you're most unsure about, ask the same question to a second AI. If
the corrections agree, reliability goes up; if they diverge, you have a
candidate to verify first. It's not a guarantee, because two AIs can
hallucinate the same thing.
:::

When to verify against the textbook depends on the correction. For small
ones (a date, a name, a quote) jot them down on the fly and check them
in a block afterwards. For big conceptual ones (a thesis attributed to
one author instead of another) stop right away and open the book: if you
revise for an hour leaning on a wrong correction, you fix the error.

## What NOT to do

**Don't revise on AI's answers instead of on the textbook.** An AI
correction has to be verified against the book before you make it yours.
If you state an error with confidence in the prompt, AI tends to follow
you.

**Don't import a deck of flashcards without checking the first ten.**
Wrong cards repeated with spaced repetition fix the error in place of
the concept.

**Don't treat the AI's "grade" as predictive.** A simulated B+ means
you had a good conversation for forty minutes with a system designed
to accommodate you. The grade is there to close the session, not to
make predictions.
