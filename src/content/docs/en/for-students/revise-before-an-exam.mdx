You've studied the syllabus with the techniques from [*Study a new
topic*](/en/for-students/study-a-new-topic/) and composed the material
with those from [*Notes from lectures and long
texts*](/en/for-students/notes-from-lectures/). Two weeks left until
the exam. You know the material, but the professor will ask you about
it on your feet, under pressure, at their pace.

Studying and revising are two different jobs. Studying is
understanding the first time. Revising is repeating what you already
know until the answer comes out fluently under exam conditions. The
textbook alone doesn't generate those conditions: you open it, you
read, you nod along. AI does: you can put it in the professor's shoes
and have it question you as many times as you want, for free, at
three in the morning if needed.

## The three techniques

### 1. Simulated oral exam

The central move of revision. The core prompt:

> *"Pretend to be my [subject] professor at the oral exam. Here's the
> course syllabus: [paste the textbook index or the list of topics].
> Ask me one question at a time, don't move to the next one until I've
> answered. After my answer, give me an honest verdict (accurate /
> imprecise / missing), and if it's imprecise tell me what you would
> have wanted to hear. Don't move to the final grade until I ask you
> to."*

Three specifications are what matters. **One question at a time**:
without this, AI fires four questions in a block, you answer
sketchily, and you lose the pressure of the face-to-face. **Honest
verdict on every answer**: by default AI wants to please, and without
explicit instruction it accepts vagueness with a "good answer, let's
move on". **No final grade until you ask for it**: without this, after
three questions it closes with a B+ and you walk away happy; with the
clause the questioning goes on until you decide, like a real oral
exam.

The syllabus pasted into the prompt prevents AI from pulling topics
from Wikipedia that your course doesn't cover. If the textbook index
is long, the list of chapters covered in class is enough. If the
course has no single textbook (slides + handouts + a few articles, a
common situation in social sciences and humanities), reconstruct the
list of topics from lecture and slide titles. Fifteen to twenty
concise entries are enough to define the perimeter.

Practical pace of the session. A good simulated oral session lasts
forty minutes, five to eight questions. No more in the same round:
after eight questions your attention drops and AI starts recycling
similar questions. Two forty-minute sessions on different days work
better than one two-hour marathon. You know you're ready on a chapter
when you answer three questions in a row without AI having to correct
you on anything substantial; below that threshold, go back to the
textbook before redoing the oral.

### 2. Written test with correction

For written exams, the pattern is the same but the loop changes: you
produce a text, AI reads it and corrects you at the point where you
went wrong, not in general.

> *"Give me a typical [subject] written exam prompt on chapter X. I'll
> answer it, then you give me an honest verdict: where I wrote
> correct things, where I was imprecise, what I should have added. If
> I got a specific step wrong, show me the correct step, not just the
> final result."*

It works the same way for technical problems in math, physics,
chemistry, engineering. The important difference is asking for the
correction of the **step**, not just the result. Knowing that "the
result is 12 and not 14" doesn't help you avoid the same mistake
again; knowing that you misapplied the chain rule at the third step
does.

For multiple-choice written tests (fifty questions in two hours,
A/B/C/D) the pattern shifts slightly: ask AI to give you ten or
fifteen questions in the style of your exam, with four alternatives
each and the answer marked. You answer before looking at the
solution, and for each wrong one ask *why* the others were wrong, not
just why the right one was right. Without the explanation of *why
not* the distractors, multiple-choice becomes pattern memorization
and you don't grasp the structure of the question.

### 3. Mind maps and flashcards

Two tools complementary to questioned revision.

The **mind map** is a hierarchical diagram: main concept at the top,
sub-concepts branching out. It's useful before the oral exam to "see"
the structure of the chapter at a glance. Ask: *"give me a textual
mind map of chapter X, main concept at the top, four or five
branches, two or three sub-concepts per branch."* The output is an
indented outline. If you prefer the graphical version, paste it into
XMind, MindMeister, or Excalidraw.

**Flashcards** are the opposite of the map: no structure, many short
questions on single concepts, to be repeated at growing intervals of
time (the principle of *spaced repetition*: you review a card when
you're about to forget it, so each repetition costs less than the
previous one).

:::tip[Flashcards: Anki, Quizlet, and the CSV format]
**Anki** and **Quizlet** are the two standard tools. Anki is free,
open source, and shows you the spaced repetition algorithm
transparently; Quizlet is friendlier graphically, with a more limited
free version. Both import flashcards from text files: the base format
is `Question,Answer` (CSV) or `Question<TAB>Answer` (TSV), one card
per line.

Ask AI: *"give me twenty flashcards on chapter X in CSV format, one
per line, comma as separator, no headers. If an answer contains
commas, wrap it in double quotes. Focus on definitions, dates, names,
and key formulas; avoid generic oral-exam questions."* You copy the
output, save it as `.csv`, import it.

Real limit: AI tends to ask questions that are too easy ("Who was
Kant?") or too open-ended ("Explain the categorical imperative").
Check the first ten cards before importing everything; if they don't
work, send AI back with an example of a good question against a bad
one.
:::

## Strategy for the professor

Two typical profiles. The techniques stay the same, the calibration
changes.

**The predictable professor.** Every year asks about the same topics,
with a recognizable type of question (precise definitions,
applications to cases, links between authors). If you have a list of
questions from past exams, even just the ones circulating in the
course's WhatsApp groups, paste it in: *"this is the style of
questions the professor asks. When you question me, simulate it."*
It's the same calibration as the third technique of [*Study a new
topic*](/en/for-students/study-a-new-topic/), carried over into
revision.

**The unpredictable professor.** Asks reasoning questions, not memory
ones: knowing chapter 4 by heart won't save you, they'll ask you to
link it to chapter 7. Here AI is useful for generating **transversal**
questions: *"give me five questions that connect chapter 4 with
chapter 7, the kind a professor would ask to see if you've integrated
the syllabus."* They won't be perfect, but they force you to build
bridges that you wouldn't build reading linearly.

And if it's the first exam with that professor and you have no past
questions or hints about their style? Go with the unpredictable
profile. It's the most defensive strategy: you prepare for connecting
questions, which require knowing the syllabus more deeply, and so
they also cover the case where the professor turns out to be the
predictable type.

## A philosophy oral exam

Student of theoretical philosophy, exam in a week. The professor is
predictable, insists on Heidegger and in particular on the concept of
*aletheia*. First thing: set up the core prompt of Technique 1 with
the course syllabus and the simulation starts. AI asks the first
question, the student answers. The exchange that follows.

<ChatDemo
  user={`For Heidegger, aletheia is the Greek concept of truth. The word literally means "unconcealment", and for Heidegger truth is not the correspondence between a proposition and a thing, but the way being manifests itself. Truth understood as idea, in the Platonic sense, is a consequence of this originary manifestation.`}
  ai={`**Accurate** on two points: the etymology of *aletheia* as "unconcealment" (more precisely *a-letheia*, privative of *lethe*, concealment) and the critique of truth as correspondence.

**Imprecise** on the last part. You said that "truth understood as idea, in the Platonic sense, is a consequence of this originary manifestation". Here you trip on one of the points the professor always comes back to. Heidegger doesn't say that Platonic truth as *idea* derives from *aletheia*: he says it *replaces* it, and that this replacement is the first fall of Western metaphysics. For Heidegger, Plato is the moment when truth stops being *disclosure* (event, happening of being) and becomes *adequation* of a gaze to an ideal form. The originary *aletheia* is covered over by the idea, not prolonged in it. You should have said: "truth as *disclosure* is what Plato, according to Heidegger, loses by introducing the idea as the measure of the true". Not a small difference: in your formulation Plato is an heir to Heidegger, in the correct formulation he is the beginning of his polemical target.

When you're ready I'll move on to the second question.`}
/>

Three things to notice. The verdict isn't a generic "good answer":
it's split into accurate / imprecise, with the imprecise zone pointed
out down to the millimeter. The correction doesn't just say "no, it's
the opposite": it tells you *what you should have said*, with the
alternative formulation. And AI stops: it doesn't move to the second
question until the student asks. The simulation holds.

This is the difference between using AI as a revision tutor and as a
serial question generator: the value isn't the number of questions
asked, it's the detail of the correction on each one.

:::caution[AI that confirms you is the risk]
AI tends to agree with you. If you answer something vague but that
sounds "in the ballpark", the default response is "yes, you got the
point", followed by an addition that fills in the gaps you left. You
walk out of the questioning convinced you knew, but you knew vaguely:
the pieces you were missing, it put them in.

Three countermeasures. The first: in the initial prompt, explicitly
ask for a strict verdict, not an encouraging one (*"if I answer
vaguely, tell me it's vague, don't fill in the gaps yourself"*). The
second, and most important: the key points where AI corrected you
need to be re-checked against the textbook. An AI correction that
sounds right to you can be a hallucination, especially on authors,
quotes, dates. The same rule applies as in [*When to trust it and
when not*](/en/fundamentals/when-to-trust-it/): cross-check with the
source on the points you'll bring to the exam.

The third, optional but useful: for the points you're most unsure
about, ask the same question to a second AI (Claude if you used
ChatGPT, Gemini if you used Claude). If the two corrections agree,
that's a higher signal of reliability. If they diverge, you've found
a candidate to verify first against the textbook. It's not a
guarantee (two AIs can hallucinate the same thing), but it raises the
bar over a single source.
:::

When to verify against the textbook, in real time or at the end of
the session? For pointwise corrections (a date, a name, a quote) it's
better to jot them down on the fly during the session and verify them
in a block afterwards. For big conceptual corrections (a thesis
attributed to one author instead of another, a philosophical
distinction) it's better to stop right away and open the book: if you
revise for an hour leaning on a wrong correction, you fix the error.

## What NOT to do

**Don't revise on AI's answers instead of on the textbook.** An AI
correction has to be verified against the book before you make it
yours. AI confirms errors if you state them with confidence in the
prompt: it tends to follow you.

**Don't import a deck of flashcards without checking the first ten.**
Wrong cards repeated with spaced repetition fix the error in place of
the concept.

**Don't treat the AI's "grade" as predictive.** A simulated B+ means
you had a good conversation for forty minutes with a system designed
to accommodate you. The grade is there to close the session, not to
make predictions.
