<LessonSummary>

- Course material isn't a single PDF: it's a mix of the professor's voice, slides, textbook, your own notes. The challenge is composing them, not summarizing one piece.
- For the professor's voice: automatic transcription (Whisper, Otter, Tactiq, Apple Voice Memos). For slides and handwritten notes: photos. For the textbook: a paragraph pasted in or pages photographed.
- The output you want is a **study map**: it holds the pieces together and tells you where each thing comes from.

You have the material, but it's everywhere. The professor's voice is
in an audio file on your phone, the slides in a PDF from the portal,
the chapter in the paper textbook, your own notes in a notebook. The
sources don't say the same thing: the professor emphasizes points that
the slides cut and the textbook expands.

When you sit down to study, the challenge is **composing them**:
understanding where they talk about the same thing and where they
diverge. In [*Summarize a long
document*](/en/everyday-use/summarize-a-long-document/) you saw how to
summarize a single text. Here you need a study map that works as your
compass.

## Case 1: the professor's voice

Automatic audio transcription is one of the areas where AI is mature
and often free. Practical tools:

- **OpenAI Whisper**: free, you use it with a desktop app. Many chat
  apps also accept the audio file directly.
- **Otter.ai**, **Tactiq**, **Read.ai**: integrated with Zoom, Google
  Meet, Microsoft Teams. For online lectures they transcribe live.
- **Apple Voice Memos**: on recent iPhones/iPads (iOS 18 and later) it
  transcribes automatically and you can send the text to ChatGPT or
  Claude.

Once you have the text, you paste or upload it into the chat with
everything else. An hour of lecture is about 8000-12000 words. Current
chats handle a one-hour transcript plus the slides plus 40-50 pages of
textbook chapter in the same prompt. With a second hour of audio and
another full chapter you get close to the limit, and the AI connects
distant things worse. For more recurring material you need the Project
in Case 3.

:::caution[Recording consent and privacy]
Recording a university lecture is legally delicate. In many
jurisdictions, recording a conversation you're part of for strictly
personal use is generally allowed; broadcasting or uploading the
recording is a different matter, and in some cases unlawful. Check your
local rules if you're unsure. Good practice: ask the professor at the
start of the course if they're fine with recording for personal study.
Don't upload audio of your professor to shareable chats. For the bigger
picture, see [*What you share when you use
AI*](/en/fundamentals/privacy-basics/).
:::

:::tip[Audio transcription: what works, what doesn't]
Whisper transcribes standard English well. It struggles with thick
dialects and dirty audio. If you record on your phone from the back of
the classroom, expect to correct some terms by hand. Always check
proper names, foreign surnames, formulas and acronyms: it tends to
"normalize" toward more common words ("Husserl" becomes "Usserl",
"Schmitt" becomes "Smith"). On a lecture with constant citations of
foreign authors, plan on a pass to fix the names before studying from
the text.
:::

## Case 2: composing multiple sources

Three inputs from the same course, one study session: slides, textbook
chapter, handwritten notes. Photograph them with your phone, in focus
and well lit, one page per shot. AI reads legible handwriting well and,
if it can't read a word, it tells you instead of guessing. On photos in
chat see [*Photos, images and files*](/en/everyday-use/images-and-files/).
The core prompt is this:

> *"I'm uploading three things: the slides of lecture 7 of Modern
> History, my handwritten notes from class (photos), and chapter 12
> of the Sabbatucci/Vidotto textbook. I want a study map that holds
> the three together, highlighting where the professor in class
> emphasized things that the textbook treats differently or doesn't
> treat at all. For each entry in the map, tell me which source it
> comes from, with tags `[Prof]`, `[Textbook]`, `[Slides]`."*

You're asking for something different from a summary: a unified
structure of the topic, in which each entry says where it comes from.
Instead of three separate summaries, a map with the threads that
connect them.

The tags in square brackets show at a glance where each source weighs
in. An entry with all three tags is the heart of the topic. An entry
with only `[Prof]` is what the professor said out loud and the others
skip: probably something that will come up at the oral exam. An entry
with only `[Textbook]` is detail from the book that the professor cut.

The natural output is a hierarchical outline of four or five sections
with two or three sub-entries each. A full example follows below.

:::caution[Verify the tags before trusting them]
The AI writes the tags, and it gets things wrong: it can attribute to
the textbook something that was only in the slides, or the other way
around. If you see a lone `[Prof]` on a point you remember reading in
the textbook, open the chapter again. For a more systematic check, take
two or three lone `[Prof]` entries, the most important ones for the oral
exam, and look in the transcript to see whether the professor actually
said them.
:::

## Case 3: organizing an entire course

When the material from a course piles up (ten sets of slides, two
textbooks, a few recordings) reloading everything in every chat is a
chore. The right tool is the **Project**: on Claude and ChatGPT it's
called "Projects", on Gemini "Gems", and ChatGPT also has Custom GPTs.
It's explained in [*Reuse context with
projects*](/en/at-work/reuse-context-with-projects/). Practical note for
the student: Claude Projects and ChatGPT Projects are also on the free
plans, with limits on the number of projects and files. Creating a
Custom GPT needs a paid plan. Gemini Gems (which become "skills" on
personal accounts from November 2026) work on the free tier too. Check
in the settings what your plan offers.

You create one project per course ("Modern History 2026") and load
all the material in once. Every chat opened inside the project links
the whole corpus. When lecture 11 comes in, you add it and that's it.
When the exam approaches, you ask questions that cut across the whole
semester: "in which lectures did the professor talk about topic X?".

## An example: Italy 1943-1946

A modern history student has three inputs for the lecture on "Italy
1943-1946": the audio transcript (45 minutes, from Whisper), the
professor's slides (10 pages) and the textbook chapter (40 pages). She
opens the chat and asks for the study map.

The map is short, five sections with two or three sub-entries: an
outline you can keep in your head. The tags track the source, and the
final observations tell you what to do with the map. With the transcript
you can also ask for a targeted lookup ("at what minute does the
professor talk about the Verona Manifesto?") instead of re-listening to
45 minutes.

## What NOT to do

**Don't turn your own notes into something you no longer recognize.**
The study map has to resemble your material. If you end up with an
outline that has nothing of your way of organizing points, you've
delegated too much: put your own emphases back in.

**Don't use the transcript as the primary source if you have the
textbook.** The professor in class speaks off the cuff, skips steps,
gets dates wrong. The textbook is edited and precise. The transcript is
useful for what the professor adds of their own (readings by other
authors, examples, emphases) and for the order in which they handle the
topics. When the sources differ on a verifiable fact (a date, a
citation, a legal article), take the textbook and add a margin note:
"the professor said X, the book says Y". At the oral exam you read the
room.
