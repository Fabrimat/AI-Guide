At the start of the module we set a contract: AI is a study tool, not
a substitute. Since then you've learned to use it to study a new
topic, take notes, revise, write a thesis, handle sources, translate,
and notice when it tricks you. One question is left, the one the
module held back: where does help end and copying begin?

The answer is a method of judgement. The principle comes from [*What
NOT to do*](/en/everyday-use/what-not-to-do/), in the part about work
where your signature counts as your competence: whoever signs is
whoever answers for it. Applied to study, it becomes a four-level
scale, a practice of declaration, and a rule that holds even when no
one is watching.

## Four levels of use

The line between help and copying is a scale. The higher you go, the
more AI substitutes your work instead of accompanying it.

### 1. Explanation and tutor

Understanding a topic, asking questions, having a line of reasoning
corrected, simulating an oral. **Always OK**, no declaration: it's like
asking a coursemate or watching a lecture video. The result (having
learned) sits in your head, not in a file you hand in. It's the method
of [*Study a new topic*](/en/for-students/study-a-new-topic/) and
[*Revise before an exam*](/en/for-students/revise-before-an-exam/).

### 2. Organization and structure

A study map of your notes, the index of a paper, the chapter outline of
a thesis. **Usually OK, but in a thesis or paper you declare it.** The
conceptual structure of the topic is yours, AI gives you the container.
For personal notes nobody asks for a note. For a thesis, one line at
the beginning or end of the document is enough. You see it in [*Notes
from lectures and long texts*](/en/for-students/notes-from-lectures/)
and in [*Write a thesis*](/en/for-students/write-a-thesis/).

### 3. Generation of portions of text

A paragraph, a transition, a conclusion, the rephrasing of an unclear
passage. **It depends on the context, and when it's allowed it has to
be declared.**

- **Thesis.** Usually forbidden, or allowed only with an analytical
  declaration: which sections, which prompts, which model version.
- **Academic paper.** The text has to be yours. AI as a style reviewer
  (flags awkward sentences, suggests synonyms) is generally OK. AI as
  the author of paragraphs isn't. The practical line: if you decide
  every substitution yourself, it's revision; if you accept AI's text
  in blocks and polish it, you're already generating.
- **Graded essay.** That's plagiarism: generating pieces and passing
  them off as yours is copying.
- **Ungraded work** (a post for the course blog, an email to a study
  group). The rule of work applies, which you'll find in Module 3.

### 4. Full generation

AI writes, you correct it and rephrase a bit. **In a formal assessment
this is copying**, however much you clean up the output. A competent
examiner notices: a fluency that isn't yours, off-register vocabulary,
an argument you can't defend when they ask "why did you write this
here?".

AI detectors aren't reliable (more below), so the risk of getting
caught isn't the point. The point is that you haven't learned what the
professor wanted. Rewriting the draft word by word changes nothing: the
argumentative structure stays AI's, and it's the part you were meant to
build.

## Declaring your use

More and more universities ask for a short note at the beginning or
end of a thesis or paper, where you declare what you did with AI and
what you didn't. Where it isn't required, declaring is still good
practice: it protects you at the defense and makes the work
verifiable.

A template formula, to adapt to your case:

> In the writing of this work, ChatGPT (version [...], model [...]) and Claude (version [...], model [...]) were used as:
> 1. study support (explanations, simulated quizzing, revision);
> 2. second readers for style revision, clarity suggestions, simulation of objections from the supervisor;
> 3. formatting of bibliographic citations (manually verified through DOI).
>
> Generative AI was NOT used for:
> 1. generating finished paragraphs or conclusions;
> 2. producing the bibliography (sources were found and verified via Scopus, JSTOR and Google Scholar);
> 3. translating or rephrasing passages without the author's intervention.

Fill the `[...]` with what you see in the interface at the time of use,
in the format *model name and date* (for example *ChatGPT, model shown
in the app, 12 May 2026*). If you used the product across different
months and the models changed, you write that.

:::note[University rules change]
Many universities already have rules on AI use, and they change often:
some have general principles for teaching, others a code of conduct or a
declaration form to attach to the thesis. One checkable example is
Stanford, which treats AI like help from another person unless the
instructor says otherwise. I don't link specific pages: before writing
your thesis, look up the rule on your university's site (usually under
"research ethics", "code of conduct" or "graduation work regulations")
and ask your supervisor. If you can't find an explicit rule, the general
rule applies: use AI as a study tool, declare what it did, don't let AI
write what you sign.
:::

A common misunderstanding is "if I declare it, it's allowed". Declaring
is a duty of transparency, and the permission comes from your
university's or instructor's rule. If the rule forbids AI for
generating text, a note admitting you did it documents the violation.

:::caution[Proctored exams and AI detectors]
**During a supervised exam, AI is forbidden**, even if the exam is
at home with a webcam and the university doesn't block ChatGPT or
Claude. It holds for written exams, online orals and timed tests. If
they ask you to do it on your own, you do it on your own.

**AI detectors** (Turnitin AI, GPTZero, Copyleaks and similar) exist,
and some universities use them. They're unreliable. Turnitin itself
writes in its disclaimer that the score shouldn't be the only basis for
an academic integrity decision. They give false positives on human
texts with a regular style (people who write cleanly and in a
structured way look like an AI) and false negatives on AI text
rewritten by hand.

If your filter is "which work is mine", detectors and the strategies to
get around them become irrelevant.
:::

## The "whoever signs" rule

The rule from [*What NOT to do*](/en/everyday-use/what-not-to-do/)
applies in three situations:

- **You take the oral.** If AI did the work, you fall apart at the
  first exchange: the professor asks you to argue a point from your
  paper and the conversation collapses in two minutes.
- **You defend the thesis.** A direct question about the third
  chapter, you can't answer, and the committee understands who wrote
  it.
- **You pass the exam.** If you studied a dumbed-down version from AI
  instead of the textbook, you show up with a preparation the professor
  fails. You invested time in the wrong place.

## What NOT to do

- **Don't have AI write what you'll sign** (exam, thesis, paper). The
  signature declares that the work is yours.
- **Don't use detectors to "optimize" AI output** until it passes.
  You're trying to fool the system, and any detector update can unmask
  a text that used to pass.
- **Don't count on "no one will know".** People who correct theses for
  a living see the pattern: prose smoother than the notes you sent,
  perfect transitions where there are usually jumps, conclusions that
  read like a Wikipedia summary.
